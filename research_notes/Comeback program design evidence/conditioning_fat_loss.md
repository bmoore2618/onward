# Conditioning (cardio) alongside 3 weekly strength sessions for deconditioned adults — fat loss and general fitness over 10–12 weeks

Scope: healthy, deconditioned adults ~30–55, primary goal fat loss (~15–20 lb) plus general fitness, 3 strength sessions/week, 75-day (~11-week) generated program, with a cardio-only track. Everything below is training guidance, not medical advice. Sources marked "practitioner synthesis" or "secondary summary" are not primary research.

Research-process note: PubMed pages were blocked (cookie wall) during this session, so several landmark abstracts were pulled through Europe PMC's REST API; those citations link to Europe PMC. The Keating 2017 PDF was fetched but not machine-readable; its numbers come from the Europe PMC abstract.

## Key question 1: Zone 2 / moderate steady-state vs. HIIT vs. mixed for fat loss, fitness and adherence in deconditioned adults

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses in overweight/inactive adults consistently find that HIIT and moderate continuous cardio (MICT) produce the same, modest fat loss; HIIT's only clear advantage is time (and a larger VO2max gain), while combined strength + cardio beats either alone for cardiometabolic outcomes. Adherence is similar between HIIT and MICT in supervised studies and drops for both when unsupervised, so the practical answer for a comeback app is "mixed, with the mode the user will actually do".

### Cited Findings
- Wewege et al. 2017 (Obesity Reviews) pooled 13 studies / 786 overweight-obese adults: HIIT and MICT produced near-identical fat loss (about 2.6 kg each; SMD 0.82 vs 0.85) and HIIT needed ~40% less training time — [secondary summary of Wewege 2017](https://getfitcraft.com/science/hiit-vs-steady-state) (practitioner site; primary paper is Wewege, van den Berg, Ward & Keech, Obes Rev 2017;18:635-646)
- Keating, Johnson, Mielke & Coombes 2017 (Obesity Reviews), 31 studies, interventions >= 4 weeks: within-group body-fat % change was -1.26% (HIIT/SIT) vs -1.48% (MICT) and fat mass -1.38 kg vs -0.91 kg; "no differences between HIIT/SIT and MICT for any body fat outcome"; comparisons with lower time commitment tended to favour MICT (p = 0.09); conclusion: HIIT/SIT gives similar fat-loss benefit to MICT "although not necessarily in a more time-efficient manner", and neither produced clinically meaningful short-term fat loss — [Europe PMC abstract, PMID 28513103](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:28513103&resultType=core&format=json)
- Batrakoulis et al. 2022 (Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes) network meta-analysis of 81 RCTs / 4,331 adults with overweight/obesity (59% female, mean age 38.7 +/- 12.3): combined aerobic + resistance training was the most effective modality for cardiometabolic health and hybrid-type training second; single-mode continuous endurance, interval or resistance training ranked lower; authors recommend "multicomponent exercise interventions" — [Penn State record of Batrakoulis 2022](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/comparative-efficacy-of-5-exercise-types-on-cardiometabolic-healt/)
- Helgerud et al. 2007 (MSSE; older landmark study): 40 moderately trained men, 3x/week for 8 weeks, four work-matched protocols; 4x4-min intervals at 90-95% HRmax with 3-min recovery raised VO2max 7.2% (55.5 to 60.4 mL/kg/min) and 15/15 intervals 5.5%, significantly more than long slow distance (70% HRmax) or lactate-threshold (85% HRmax) running (P < 0.01); stroke volume rose ~10% after interval training — [Europe PMC abstract, PMID 17414804](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:17414804&resultType=core&format=json)
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of HIIT compliance (188 studies, 8,928 participants): supervised compliance averaged 89.4% for HIIT vs 92.5% for MICT; unsupervised adherence 63% (HIIT) vs 68.2% (MICT); across 65 meta-analysed studies, compliance did not differ between supervised HIIT and MICT — [CSEP summary of Santos et al. meta-analysis](https://csep.ca/2025/05/01/time-to-end-uncivil-debates-high-intensity-interval-training-hiit-is-just-as-enjoyable-and-likely-to-be-adhered-to-as-moderate-intensity-continuous-training-mict/); same figures in the [IJBNPA paper (Santos et al. 2023)](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com:443/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12966-023-01535-w.pdf)
- Affective response: experiences *during* HIIT tend to be less positive than during MICT, while *post-exercise* enjoyment of HIIT is generally higher; among inactive and overweight adults, intensity above the ventilatory threshold has a negative effect on in-session affect, and lower-intensity or shorter intervals improve pleasure — [CSEP 2025](https://csep.ca/2025/05/01/time-to-end-uncivil-debates-high-intensity-interval-training-hiit-is-just-as-enjoyable-and-likely-to-be-adhered-to-as-moderate-intensity-continuous-training-mict/); [Tempest et al. review PDF, Northumbria](https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/46483/1/Tempest_PMS-21-0010.R2.jdb.%5B1%5D.pdf)
- Wilson et al. 2012 (older landmark meta-analysis) also found that fat-loss effect size correlated with aerobic intensity (% max HR, r = -0.60), i.e. higher-intensity cardio tended to lose more fat inside concurrent programs — [Europe PMC abstract, PMID 22002517](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:22002517&resultType=core&format=json)

### Inferences
- For an app whose promise is "consistency over perfection", the evidence supports making moderate/Zone 2 work the default (lower in-session discomfort for inactive users, equal fat loss) and layering in short intervals for time-efficiency and VO2max, rather than selling HIIT as a fat-loss shortcut.
- Expected fat loss from cardio alone over 10-12 weeks is small (roughly 1-3 kg in trials); a 15-20 lb goal will depend mostly on nutrition and total activity (steps), with training mainly preserving muscle and building fitness. Copy should set that expectation.
- Because combined training ranked best (Batrakoulis), the 3-strength + 2-conditioning structure is well aligned with the evidence.

### Gaps
- No Andreato meta-analysis was retrieved this session (Andreato et al. 2019, Obes Rev, HIIT vs MICT in overweight/obese adults; expected to agree with Wewege/Keating but not verified here).
- Enjoyment/adherence data specifically in unsupervised app-based settings for 30-55-year-old deconditioned users was not found; the 63-68% unsupervised adherence figures are pooled across populations.

## Key question 2: Concurrent-training interference — does cardio blunt strength/hypertrophy at these volumes, and how to separate sessions?

### Takeaway
At 2-3 cardio sessions per week, cardio does not blunt maximal strength or hypertrophy; the only measurable cost is to explosive power, and mainly when cardio is done in the same session or within ~3 h of lifting. Running shows more interference than cycling in the older literature.

### Cited Findings
- Schumann et al. 2022 (Sports Medicine) updated meta-analysis, 43 RCTs / >1,000 participants: hypertrophy SMD between concurrent and strength-only training was -0.01 (95% CI -0.16 to 0.18, p = 0.919); no compromise of maximal strength; explosive strength showed a modest negative effect (SMD -0.28), mainly when aerobic and strength work were in the same session with < 3 h between; interference did not depend on cycling vs running, > 5 vs < 5 weekly sessions, untrained vs active, age < 40 vs > 40, or same-session vs same-day vs different-day — [Schumann et al. 2022 PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Compatibility-of-Concurrent-Aerobic-and-Strength-Training.pdf); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/37)
- Wilson et al. 2012 (JSCR; older landmark meta-analysis): effect sizes for hypertrophy 0.85 (concurrent) vs 1.23 (strength alone); strength 1.44 vs 1.76; power 0.55 vs 0.91; concurrent training with running, but not cycling, caused significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength; negative correlations between endurance frequency (r = -0.26 to -0.35) and session duration (r = -0.29 to -0.75) and the hypertrophy/strength/power effect sizes — [Europe PMC abstract, PMID 22002517](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:22002517&resultType=core&format=json)
- Practitioner synthesis consistent with the above: separate sessions by several hours, or put lifting first when they must be combined; keep cardio volume moderate — [train.fitness practitioner blog](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/mix-strength-and-cardio-without-losing) (practitioner source, not primary)

### Inferences
- With 2-3 cardio sessions of 20-40 min per week, interference is not a real concern for a fat-loss/general-fitness user; the generator can safely put cardio on non-lifting days, or after lifting on the same day, without special rules beyond "lift first".
- Cycling (Peloton) and walking are the lowest-interference modalities; heavy-bag rounds are impact-free for the legs and should behave more like cycling than running, though this is an inference, not a tested claim.
- Explosive-power loss is irrelevant to this user population's goals; the app does not need to protect it.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tested interference specifically in deconditioned 30-55-year-olds using 3 strength + 2 cardio sessions; the meta-analyses' subgroup results (untrained, >40 y, different days) are the closest evidence.
- Long-term (> 12 week) concurrent data in this population were not located.

## Key question 3: Weekly dose and ramping a deconditioned adult from near zero to 150-300 min over 10-12 weeks; interval progressions; safety screening framing

### Takeaway
Guidelines set 150-300 min/week moderate (or 75-150 vigorous) plus 2 strength days, but stress that "any amount is better than none". ACSM-style progression for beginners starts at 10-20 min at light-moderate intensity (RPE 11-13), adds 5-10 min every 1-2 weeks (roughly <= 10% per week), and increases duration before intensity. Walk/run progressions like Couch to 5K show a concrete 9-week interval ramp from 1:1.5 min run/walk to 30 min continuous.

### Cited Findings
- WHO: adults should do at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week; "any amount of physical activity is better than none", "all physical activity counts", and muscle strengthening benefits everyone — [WHO physical activity fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity) (the fact sheet references the 2020 WHO Global guidelines, which specify 150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous plus 2 strength days; the full guideline was not fetched this session)
- CDC (US guidelines): adults need 150 min moderate activity and 2 days of muscle-strengthening per week; the 150 min can be split (e.g. 30 min x 5 days); "some physical activity is better than none"; people who have been inactive or have overweight should talk to a doctor before starting *vigorous* activity — [CDC: Adding physical activity as an adult](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/adding-adults/index.html)
- ACSM-based progression guidance for deconditioned/novice/returning exercisers: light-to-moderate intensity (40-60% HRR, RPE 11-13 on the 6-20 scale), sessions starting at 15-20 min progressing toward 30 continuous minutes; increase intensity, duration or frequency by no more than ~10% per week; for beginners increase session duration by 5-10 min every 1-2 weeks; sedentary starters can begin at 10-15 min, 3 days/week; increase time before intensity in the initial phase to avoid fatigue and compliance drop-off — [ACSM-CPT study guide, Cardiorespiratory progression](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/acsm-cpt/chapter-5-cardiorespiratory-programming/cardiorespiratory-progression-periodization); [ACSM-CPT FITT-VP guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/acsm-cpt/chapter-5-cardiorespiratory-programming/cardiorespiratory-fitt-vp) (secondary study-guide summaries of ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription, not the guideline itself)
- Couch to 5K (NHS-style 9-week plan, 3 runs/week) as a concrete interval ramp: Week 1 = 5-min brisk walk then alternate 60 s run / 90 s walk for 20 min; Week 2 = 90 s run / 2 min walk; Week 3 = 2 x (90 s run, 90 s walk, 3 min run, 3 min walk); Week 4 = 3/1.5/5/2.5/3/1.5/5 min run-walk blocks; Week 5 ends with 20 min continuous; Week 6 ends with 25 min; Week 7 = 25 min; Week 8 = 28 min; Week 9 = 30 min continuous — [Couch to 5K week by week (Health and Social Care NI)](https://healthwell.eani.org.uk/node/2247); [PITA Couch to 5K PDF](https://www.pita.org.uk/images/Couch_to_5k_29_July_2020.pdf)
- Interval tolerance in inactive/overweight adults: intensities above the ventilatory threshold worsen in-session affect; shorter and/or lower-intensity intervals improve pleasure — [Tempest et al. review](https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/46483/1/Tempest_PMS-21-0010.R2.jdb.%5B1%5D.pdf)

### Inferences
- A plausible 11-week ramp that respects the ~10%/week and "duration before intensity" rules: Weeks 1-2: 2 x 15-20 min easy (RPE 11-12) + walking; Weeks 3-4: 2 x 20-25 min; Weeks 5-6: 2 x 25-30 min with one session adding short intervals (e.g. 6 x 30 s brisk / 90 s easy); Weeks 7-8: 2-3 x 30 min, intervals move to 8 x 45 s / 75 s; Weeks 9-11: 30-40 min, intervals 8-10 x 60 s / 60 s or 3-4 x 3-4 min at "hard but controlled". Combined with 3 strength sessions (~40 min) this lands near 150-200 min/week of structured activity by week 8, plus steps.
- The 30s/90s -> 60s/60s interval progression in the brief is consistent with the Couch to 5K pattern (short work, longer rest, then progressively equal work:rest and longer blocks) and with the affect literature (keep early intervals short).
- Safety framing: the app can mirror the CDC's non-medical wording ("if you've been inactive, check with a clinician before starting vigorous activity") and use a self-screen (e.g. PAR-Q+ style questions) without presenting itself as giving medical advice.

### Gaps
- No RCT was found that directly tested different ramp rates (e.g. 10% vs 20% per week) for injury or dropout in deconditioned adults; the 10% rule is guideline/consensus, not trial-derived.
- The specific 30s/90s -> 60s/60s interval progression has no study behind it that I could locate; it is a practitioner heuristic consistent with Couch to 5K.
- The full WHO 2020 guideline text (300-min upper bound, vigorous equivalents) was not fetched; only the fact sheet and CDC page were.

## Key question 4: Daily step targets (7,000-10,000) — evidence and interaction with training days

### Takeaway
The 2025 Lancet Public Health dose-response meta-analysis found health benefits rise steeply up to ~5,000-7,000 steps/day and flatten after; 7,000/day is called a realistic, evidence-based target, with 10,000 still reasonable for more active people. Direct evidence on how step goals should change on lifting vs cardio days was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Ding et al. 2025, The Lancet Public Health 10(8): 57 studies (35 cohorts) reviewed, 31 meta-analysed; inverse non-linear associations with inflection points around 5,000-7,000 steps/day for all-cause mortality, CVD incidence, dementia and falls; vs 2,000 steps/day, 7,000 steps/day was associated with HR 0.53 all-cause mortality (47% lower), 0.75 CVD incidence, 0.53 CVD mortality, 0.63 cancer mortality, 0.86 type 2 diabetes, 0.62 dementia, 0.78 depressive symptoms, 0.72 falls; "10,000 steps per day can still be a viable target for those who are more active" but 7,000 "might be a more realistic and achievable target for some" — [Europe PMC abstract, PMID 40713949](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:40713949&resultType=core&format=json); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40713949/)
- Secondary coverage of the same paper confirming the 7,000-step framing — [epocrates summary](https://epocrates.com/online/article/7-000-daily-steps-associated-with-substantial-reductions-in-mortality-and); [lifespan.io summary](https://lifespan.io/7000-steps-a-day-are-enough-for-most-benefits/)

### Inferences
- Onward's existing 8,000-step habit sits between the 7,000 inflection and the 10,000 convention and is defensible; a generator could start new users at ~6,000-7,000 and nudge upward, which fits the "any amount is better than none" principle.
- Steps are a largely independent, low-interference activity (walking is the least demanding modality); there is no evidence-based reason to lower the step goal on strength days, though an app may reasonably count a cardio session's steps toward the daily total.

### Gaps
- No studies were found quantifying step targets' direct effect on body-weight loss over 10-12 weeks (the Lancet paper covers mortality/disease, not weight), nor on how step goals interact with structured training days.

## Key question 5: Weekly structure examples (3 strength + 2 conditioning + 1 recovery + 1 rest) and where easy/Zone 2 days help

### Takeaway
No trial directly compares 7-day templates, but the combined-training ranking (Batrakoulis), the no-interference findings for different-day cardio (Schumann), the 150-300 min guideline, and the affect data all support a 3 strength + 2 conditioning + 1 easy-movement + 1 rest week, with the easy day doubling as recovery and step-goal insurance.

### Cited Findings
- Combined aerobic + resistance training ranked first for cardiometabolic health in overweight/obese adults (81 RCTs) — [Batrakoulis 2022](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/comparative-efficacy-of-5-exercise-types-on-cardiometabolic-healt/)
- Different-day, same-day and same-session concurrent training all left strength and hypertrophy intact; only explosive strength suffered with < 3 h separation — [Schumann 2022](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Compatibility-of-Concurrent-Aerobic-and-Strength-Training.pdf)
- 150 min/week can be split into 30 min x 5 days; 2 muscle-strengthening days per week — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/adding-adults/index.html)
- Easy, below-threshold work is more pleasant in-session for inactive adults — [Tempest et al.](https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/46483/1/Tempest_PMS-21-0010.R2.jdb.%5B1%5D.pdf)
- Helgerud: 3 sessions/week of 4x4 for 8 weeks raised VO2max ~7% in trained men; moderate continuous work matched for volume did not — [Helgerud 2007](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:17414804&resultType=core&format=json)

### Inferences
- Template A (default): Mon Strength A, Tue Conditioning 1 (easy/Zone 2 bike or walk, 20-30 min), Wed Strength B, Thu Conditioning 2 (intervals, short), Fri Strength C, Sat easy movement/recovery (walk, light bike, mobility; counts toward steps), Sun rest. This keeps every cardio bout on a non-lifting day (zero interference) and alternates hard/easy days.
- Template B (time-poor): cardio 10-20 min *after* two of the lifting days, plus one standalone 30-min easy session; same-day after-lifting cardio is supported by Schumann (no strength/hypertrophy cost).
- The Saturday easy day is the most valuable adherence lever: it has the highest affect, lowest injury demand, and lets a missed Tue/Thu session be absorbed without "restarting" — matching the Onward philosophy.

### Gaps
- No controlled comparison of 7-day layouts (e.g. 3+2+1+1 vs 3+3+1) in deconditioned adults was found; the template is an inference from component evidence.

## Key question 6: Cardio-only tracks (bike, treadmill, walking/running) for users with no strength equipment, plus bodyweight strength

### Takeaway
Couch to 5K provides a validated-in-practice 9-week run/walk ramp; Peloton's Power Zone series uses an FTP test, 5-week blocks, and a re-test; the Norwegian 4x4 is the best-documented interval format for VO2max once a base exists. For a cardio-only track, pair one of these with 2 short bodyweight sessions so the user still gets the guideline's 2 strength days.

### Cited Findings
- Couch to 5K: 9 weeks, 3 runs/week, from 60 s run / 90 s walk x ~8 (20 min) in Week 1 to 30 min continuous in Week 9; the method is alternating run and walk with run blocks lengthening and walk blocks shrinking — [Couch to 5K week by week](https://healthwell.eani.org.uk/node/2247); [PITA PDF](https://www.pita.org.uk/images/Couch_to_5k_29_July_2020.pdf)
- Peloton "Discover Your Power Zones": first-level program for beginners, 5 weeks, 25 classes, starts with an FTP test in Week 1 and ends with a re-test in Week 5; followed by "Build Your Power Zones" (5 weeks, designed to avoid plateaus) and "Peak Your Power Zones" — [Champagne and Coffee Stains guide (fan site)](https://www.champagneandcoffeestains.com/peloton-discover-your-power-zones/); [Pelobuddy: Build Your Power Zones](https://www.pelobuddy.com/build-your-power-zones-program); [What is the Peloton FTP test](https://www.leahingram.com/what-is-the-peloton-ftp-test/) (all secondary/fan sources; Peloton's own program pages were not fetched)
- Norwegian 4x4: 4 x 4 min at 85-95% HRmax with 3 min active recovery; the 2007 study used moderately trained men, 3x/week, 8 weeks, +7.2% VO2max — [Helgerud 2007 abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:17414804&resultType=core&format=json); protocol description in a [secondary summary](https://outliyr.com/reports/norwegian-4x4)
- Guideline requirement of 2 muscle-strengthening days/week applies regardless of equipment — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/adding-adults/index.html); "muscle strengthening benefits everyone" — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity)

### Inferences
- Cardio-only 11-week track (bike or treadmill or outdoor): Weeks 1-3 build duration only (15 -> 25 min easy, RPE 11-13, 3x/week), Weeks 4-6 add Couch-to-5K-style short intervals once per week, Weeks 7-9 add a second interval day and extend the easy day to 30-40 min, Weeks 10-11 introduce 3-4 x 3-4 min "hard but controlled" blocks (a scaled-down 4x4) once per week. Re-test (timed 2-km walk/run, or 20-min bike test) in Week 1 and Week 11, mirroring Peloton's FTP-test bookends.
- Add 2 x 20-min bodyweight sessions (squat/hinge/push/pull/carry patterns, progressed by reps then tempo then single-leg) on non-interval days to satisfy the 2-strength-day guideline; the brief's existing exercise-substitution rule should apply here too.
- The 4x4 at 85-95% HRmax is too demanding for weeks 1-6 of a deconditioned start given the affect data; use it only as a late-phase option, and let RPE substitute for HRmax when no heart-rate device exists.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of Couch to 5K outcomes or dropout was located in this session (only program descriptions).
- Peloton's program structures come from fan/blog sites, not Peloton documentation; class counts and durations may have changed.
- The 4x4 evidence base here is in moderately trained men; the "4x4 for the deconditioned" literature (e.g. Rognmo/Wisloff patient studies) was not fetched this session.

## Key question 7: Founder's modalities — Peloton bike, heavy bag (boxing rounds), walking: intensity and injury evidence

### Takeaway
Heavy-bag work is genuinely vigorous (about 8 kcal/min, HR ~156 bpm, roughly 75-85% HRmax in round-based sessions) and is a usable interval modality; non-contact bag training injury data are thin, with the available boxing injury literature dominated by sparring and bouts, not bag work. Cycling is the lowest-interference modality for a concurrent program.

### Cited Findings
- Games for Health Journal 2014: 30-min heavy-bag sessions of ten 3-min bouts produced 8.0 kcal/min (241 kcal total) at a heart rate of 156 bpm, measured by indirect calorimetry (young adults) — [CSU Ohio faculty record of the Wii vs heavy-bag study](https://facultyprofile.csuohio.edu/en/publications/a-comparison-of-energy-expenditure-during-wii-boxing-versus-heavy-18/)
- A 1-min bout of heavy-bag punching (96 punches, 16 oz gloves) cost ~36.7 kcal in total when aerobic, anaerobic and recovery components were summed (aerobic exercise cost 7.9 kcal, anaerobic 8.9 kcal) — [European Journal of Human Movement 2019](https://doaj.org/article/78866bc1f99f4d51b13fcba1356fc61f)
- Secondary summaries report heart rates of 75-85% of max during active intervals of a 30-min bag session and MET estimates of ~6-12 depending on intensity and rest — [sportssurge summary](https://sportssurge.alibaba.com/boxing/how-many-calories-does-boxing-burn) (secondary, unverified against the primary JSCR paper)
- Boxing injury literature: reported training injury rates of 12.8 per 1,000 h in one study and 2.0 per 1,000 h overall in another; in a Portuguese sample, 75% of boxers had no injury; sparring accounted for 34.3% of injuries; head and upper-limb injuries most common; training injuries are less common and less severe than bout injuries — [Int J Sports Med 2015 abstract](https://thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-0034-1387764); [BJSM 2006 boxing injury review](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/40/8/670); [Portuguese boxing injury study](https://repositorio.ipcb.pt/entities/publication/849304ec-dbd6-4468-873b-e09cb3d6f6c3)
- Cycling produced no interference with hypertrophy/strength in the older meta-analysis, whereas running did — [Wilson 2012](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:22002517&resultType=core&format=json)

### Inferences
- Heavy-bag rounds are a natural interval format: 2-3 min rounds at RPE 14-16 with 60-90 s rest, starting at 4-6 rounds and building to 8-10, fit the same ramp logic as bike intervals and give the founder a non-cycling vigorous option.
- Because the available injury data concern sparring and competition, the main practical risks for solo bag work are hand/wrist (wrap and glove), shoulder overuse, and rotational load through the trunk; the latter is relevant to a user with a lumbar-fusion flag, so the generator should offer the bike as the default swap when that profile flag is set (this is a training-adjustment inference, not medical guidance).
- Walking is the recovery/steps modality; the Peloton bike is the best default for Zone 2 and for post-lift same-day cardio because of its low interference profile.

### Gaps
- No study of injury rates in non-contact, recreational heavy-bag or "boxing fitness" classes was found; the figures above come from competitive/sparring boxing and overstate bag-only risk.
- The primary JSCR heavy-bag intensity study (75-85% HRmax claim) was not retrieved; only a secondary summary cites it.
- No data on heavy-bag training and spinal loading or back-injury risk were found.
