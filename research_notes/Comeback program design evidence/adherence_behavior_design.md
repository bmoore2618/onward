# Adherence and behavior-design evidence for a 75-day "comeback" fitness app

Scope: healthy deconditioned adults (~30–55) restarting exercise after a long break. Evidence favours 2015–2026 sources; landmark older work is flagged. Research date: 2026-10-06.

A note on sources: several peer-reviewed PDFs (the Couch-to-5K paper, MDPI/Europe PMC mirrors) returned 403 or unparseable binary, so some findings for those papers rest on search-result abstracts and secondary summaries rather than a full read. These are flagged where relevant.

---

## Key Question 1: Session length, weekly frequency, program length and dropout timing

### Takeaway
Most dropout happens early, in the first 2–8 weeks and often during the "ramp-up" before a programme reaches its full prescribed dose; once people get through the ramp, adherence tends to stay flat. For beginners the minimum effective dose of strength training is small (even one short session a week produces gains in the first ~12 weeks), so a comeback programme can afford to start well below what a former athlete thinks they "should" do.

### Cited Findings
- STRRIDE trials (947 adults, 3 RCTs, 6–8 month exercise interventions): 31% dropped out. Approximately 67% of dropouts discontinued "prior to the start of or while ramping up to the prescribed exercise volume and intensity." Ramp-up phases lasted 8–12 weeks. Top reason for dropout: lack of time (40%); then lost to follow-up (18%), exacerbation of a prior injury (12%), health issues (10%), changed mind (9%). Dropouts were on average heavier (BMI 31.0 vs 30.2) and less fit at baseline (VO2peak 25.0 vs 26.7). Authors recommend targeting support at the "adoption period" (first 2–3 months) and asking whether ramp-up prescriptions are "too lofty for sedentary individuals" — [Collins et al., STRRIDE dropout analysis, PMC9165469](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9165469/); summarised by [ACSM blog](https://www.acsm.org/blog-detail/acsm-blog/2022/04/19/what-makes-an-exercise-program-stick-the-strride-randomized-trials)
- In STRRIDE, "among individuals who completed the ramp training period, subsequent exercise intervention adherence did not waiver over the ensuing 6–8 months of training" — [search summary of the STRRIDE paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9165469/)
- A large exercise-referral dataset (6,894 participants, 12-week scheme): 37.8% dropped out within 6 weeks and 50.0% by week 12; smoking and higher-risk referral tier predicted dropout — [Durham repository (exercise referral scheme analysis)](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1395237) (search-result summary; full paper not read)
- Couch-to-5K (modified 9-week programme, UK, Edge Hill University): only 27.3% completed; almost three-quarters dropped out, "mostly between weeks two and five". 21 injuries reported (19% of participants); previous injury raised the odds of a new injury (OR 7.56, 95% CI 2.06–27.75). Interview themes: musculoskeletal injury, negative emotions linked to non-completion, and the design of the programme ("the plans progress too aggressively for true beginners"). Authors recommend injury-prevention advice and "more flexible designs" — [Couch-to-5k or Couch to Ouch to Couch!? IJERPH 2023, 20(17):6682](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/20/17/6682); [PMC10487403](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10487403/); [UCLan repository PDF](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/48883/1/ijerph-20-06682.pdf) (summaries from search results; full text returned 403)
- Minimum effective dose (narrative review, 2024): for beginners, "one weekly resistance training session is sufficient to induce strength gains ... with fewer than 3 sets and loads below 50% of 1RM" for at least the first 12 weeks; multi-joint exercises give similar or larger effects than single-joint; when volume is equated, higher frequency is "not always correlated with greater gains". The review's stated motivation is that minimal doses "may be more acceptable to inactive people or beginners" and so improve adherence — [Minimalist Training narrative review, PMC10933173](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10933173/); [sci-sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/resistance-training-minimum-dose-for-better-health-241/)
- Meta-analyses summarised in the same coverage suggest 30–60 minutes of strength training per week is associated with reduced mortality and improved health outcomes — [sci-sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/resistance-training-minimum-dose-for-better-health-241/) (secondary source)
- Strava "Year in Sport" data: athletes who upload ~5 times a week have twice as many active weeks per year as those uploading twice a week; setting a goal in the app raised activity by up to 15%, and goal-setters in January were more likely still to be active in July. Strava also popularised "Quitters' Day" (the second Friday/19 January in different years) as the date most New Year resolutions lapse — [road.cc](https://road.cc/content/news/254770-have-you-given-your-new-years-resolutions-yet); [BikeRadar](https://www.bikeradar.com/news/today-is-the-day-we-lose-motivation-strava-says); [endurance.biz](https://endurance.biz/2020/industry-news/january-19th-2020-dubbed-quitters-day-by-strava/) (company-reported, not peer reviewed)
- Fitness-app benchmark data: Day-30 retention for Health & Fitness apps is roughly 3% (AppsFlyer) to 8% (Adjust; Day-1 ~27%); iOS 3.9% vs Android 3.4% at Day 30 — [Business of Apps](https://businessofapps.com/data/health-fitness-app-benchmarks); [Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/mobile-app-retention). Note: these count installs, including people who never intended to train, so they are a floor, not a target for a paid/committed cohort.
- Gym-membership attrition quoted in the same coverage: "63% of new gym members quit before the third month, and fewer than 4% were still training a year later" — [getfitcraft summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/workout-adherence-statistics) (secondary aggregator; primary source not verified)

### Inferences
- A 75-day programme spans exactly the window where most people quit (weeks 2–8). Design effort should be front-loaded: the first 2–3 weeks should be deliberately easy, short, and almost impossible to "fail", with the real dose arriving once the routine has survived the danger zone.
- Because STRRIDE's dropouts clustered in the ramp-up and C25K's in weeks 2–5 with injury as a driver, "progress too aggressively" is the main design mistake to avoid for returners, especially former athletes whose ego and memory of past fitness may push them to start too hard.
- "Lack of time" being the top reason for quitting argues for sessions that fit a plausible slot (the founder's ~40 min sessions are reasonable; a 20–25 min fallback version of each session could rescue days that would otherwise be missed).
- The minimum-dose literature means 3 strength days/week plus walking is more than enough stimulus for a deconditioned adult; the limiting factor is adherence, not stimulus.

### Gaps
- I found no head-to-head trial of programme length (e.g. 6 vs 8 vs 12 weeks) on adherence in healthy adults; the 75-day figure has no direct evidence for or against it beyond "most dropout is earlier than that".
- No randomised evidence specifically on 30 vs 45 vs 60 minute sessions and adherence in returners; the evidence is indirect (minimum-dose reviews and "time" as the top dropout reason).
- The exercise-referral dataset (6,894 participants) is from a clinical referral population, not healthy returners; treat the 37.8%-by-6-weeks figure as directional.

## Key Question 2: Fixed vs flexible programmes, choice, autoregulation, and self-determination theory

### Takeaway
Autonomy-supportive designs and autonomous motivation are consistently linked to adherence, and the one often-cited strength-training trial of a readiness-based flexible template produced better strength (and is cited for adherence) than a fixed order. But the trials that simply offer "pick HIIT or steady-state" show no adherence benefit, so choice helps when it is bounded, low-effort and tied to how the person feels today, not when it is open-ended.

### Cited Findings
- Teixeira et al. 2012 systematic review (66 studies): "consistent support for a positive relation between more autonomous forms of motivation and exercise", with identified regulation (doing it because you value the outcome) predicting initial/short-term adoption more strongly, and intrinsic motivation (enjoyment) more predictive of long-term adherence — [Teixeira et al., IJBNPA 2012 (landmark)](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78)
- Rodrigues et al. 2018 systematic review: autonomy support from exercise professionals contributes to basic-need satisfaction and more autonomous regulation, which is "a fundamental indicator of long-term physical exercise adherence" — [Rodrigues et al., Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02141/pdf)
- McNamara & Stearne 2010 (landmark, small): college students trained 2×/week for 12 weeks; both groups had 10/15/20-rep sessions, but the flexible group picked which session to do each day from a 0–10 daily energy rating. The flexible template improved leg-press strength more than the fixed template, and the ability to influence workouts is reported as beneficial for adherence and gains — [Stronger by Science summary (Zourdos)](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=45619); [JSSM](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-15-229.xml); ["The effect of periodization on training program adherence"](https://kujane.minciencias.gov.co/Record/RCUC2_7b51391e8c4bee367a3e705f2cddaecc/Details)
- Flexible nonlinear periodization is defined as an autoregulation strategy where daily workloads "are chosen each day based on each individual's 'readiness-to-train'" — [Frontiers in Sports and Active Living 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.685813/epub)
- A three-arm pragmatic RCT in a 4-week diabetes-prevention programme found that letting people choose between HIIT and moderate continuous training "did not significantly improve perceived autonomy support, motivation regulation, free-living physical activity adherence, or cardiorespiratory fitness compared to exercise prescription" — [University of Alberta thesis/trial](https://era.library.ualberta.ca/items/82127786-244c-402a-93fd-94a8952c95a7/download/96f995ee-782e-4da0-b379-2fd2cda92d26)
- A registered trial ("Power of Choice on Autonomy, Motivation, Exercise Adherence, and Cardiorespiratory Fitness", NCT03576924) is testing exactly this question; a search summary reports a 240-participant trial where "those in a choice condition better adhered to exercise than those in a no-choice condition" — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03576924](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03576924); [protocol PDF](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/24/NCT03576924/Prot_SAP_000.pdf). I could not confirm the 240-participant result in a published paper; treat as unverified.
- A search summary described a study where a "flexible, trainee-driven" group's adherence fell over time "potentially due to decision fatigue" — I could not locate the primary paper and this claim should be treated as unverified (see Gaps).
- Broken-streak research also speaks to flexibility: participants allowed to keep a streak alive through two different activity types "showed significantly higher motivation than those required to stick with one" — [UDel / Silverman](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)

### Inferences
- The strongest-supported form of flexibility is *bounded*: a small, pre-written menu (e.g. the planned session, a shorter version, an easier version, or a movement swap) chosen from how you feel today. This matches McNamara & Stearne's "rate your energy, pick the matching session" model and the Silverman finding that multiple "ways to count" raise motivation.
- Open-ended choice (design your own session, pick any modality) has weaker support and risks decision fatigue; the diabetes-prevention trial found no benefit from a modality choice.
- For SDT: in the first weeks lean on *identified* regulation (make the "why" visible: "this is what gets you back to playing with your kids / lifting what you used to") and later surface enjoyment and competence (PRs, "you're stronger than week 1").
- Exercise swaps (already in Onward) are autonomy-supportive and consistent with the evidence provided the default is always pre-filled so the user never faces a blank screen.

### Gaps
- No direct RCT on in-app exercise swaps or "lighter day" options and adherence in deconditioned adults.
- The "decision fatigue lowered adherence in the flexible group" claim surfaced in a search summary without a locatable primary source; do not cite it as fact.
- McNamara & Stearne is a 2010 study on college students, not 30–55-year-old returners.

## Key Question 3: Habit formation, time to automaticity, habit stacking and daily non-exercise habits

### Takeaway
Exercise habits take longer to form than simple habits (Lally's exercise median was ~91 days, beyond the 84-day study), a 2024 meta-analysis puts medians at 59–66 days with means of 106–154 days and huge individual spread; missing a single day did not materially affect habit growth, though many missed days did. Consistent cues (same time/place) and implementation intentions ("when X, I do Y") have small-to-moderate but reliable effects on physical activity, and self-monitoring is the single most effective behaviour-change technique.

### Cited Findings
- Lally et al. 2010 (landmark): 96 volunteers (mean age 27), 84-day daily logging of a chosen behaviour in a consistent context. Median 66 days to reach 95% of modelled automaticity, range 18–254 days, based on only 39 participants with adequate curve fits. Exercise-type behaviours took ~1.5× longer: exercise median 91 days (vs 65 eating, 59 drinking), which *exceeded the 84-day study period* so is an extrapolation. The authors measured "automaticity ... rather than specifically habit." — [The Behavioral Scientist: what Lally actually found](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit); [UCL blog](https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/bsh/2012/06/29); [Peer Community In reply](https://rr.peercommunityin.org/download/t_recommendations.reply_pdf.8382af6b73bdd0f7.6d616e757363726970745f7072657072696e742e706466.pdf)
- Lally: "Missing a single opportunity to perform the behavior did not materially affect the overall habit formation process", whether early or late in the 12 weeks (scores dropped less than half a point and recovered). But "too many missed repeats ... even if spread out over time, had a cumulative effect, reducing the maximum automaticity level" — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit)
- 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis ("Time to form a habit", 20 studies, 2,601 participants, 8 on physical activity): four studies reported median time to habit formation of 59–66 days and mean 106–154 days, with individual variability of 4–335 days — [University of Adelaide record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006); [PMC11641623](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/)
- Meta-analysis of habit-formation interventions: they significantly increased physical-activity habit strength vs control — [Effects of habit formation interventions on PA habit strength, PMC10498635](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10498635/)
- Gardner & Lally's framework: habit formation involves deciding to act, initiating, repeating, and developing cue–behaviour associations; Gardner et al. 2011 found a moderate-to-strong habit–physical-activity correlation — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021 systematic review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.626750/full)
- Cue consistency: moderate-vigorous physical activity was predicted by habit and by cue consistency; the cue-consistency–habit link was stronger than in studies that evaluated single cues — [Testing the Effect of Cue Consistency, Behavioral Sciences 2024](https://0-www-mdpi-com.brum.beds.ac.uk/2076-328X/14/6/445)
- Implementation intentions ("when/where/how" plans): meta-analysis of 26 studies found effect size 0.31 post-intervention and 0.24 at follow-up on physical activity, larger when barrier management was included — [McMaster Optimal Aging summary](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/HE/meta-analytic-review-effect-implementation-intentions-physical-activity-25322); a separate meta-analysis of 13 RCTs found an effect of 0.25 when the plan was reinforced — [PMC6235272](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6235272/)
- Michie et al. 2009 meta-regression (122 evaluations, N = 44,747; landmark): overall pooled effect for PA/healthy-eating interventions 0.31 (95% CI 0.26–0.36). Self-monitoring explained the most between-study heterogeneity (13%); interventions combining self-monitoring with at least one other control-theory technique (goal setting, feedback, review of goals) were more effective (0.42 vs 0.26) — [Michie et al. 2009, PubMed 19916637](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/); [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/13754)
- Kaushal & Rhodes 2015 (gym members, new exercisers): exercising at least 4×/week for 6 weeks was the threshold at which exercise became habitual in their sample — [Kaushal & Rhodes 2015, UVic PDF](https://www.uvic.ca/research/labs/bmed/assets/docs/Kaushal,%20Rhodes,%202015.pdf) (search-result listing; full text not read, verify the exact threshold before quoting)

### Inferences
- 75 days sits just past the ~66-day median but short of the exercise-specific estimates (91 days; means 106–154). Framing the programme as "the foundation of a habit, not the finished habit" is more honest than "after 75 days it's automatic".
- "Missed a day? Onward." is directly supported by Lally: one miss has negligible effect on habit growth. The design should however gently discourage *clusters* of misses (e.g. after 3+ missed sessions, offer a shortened re-entry session rather than silence), since cumulative misses lower the ceiling.
- Daily non-exercise habits (steps, water, sleep, protein) are simpler behaviours that automate faster (Lally: ~59–65 days) and so give users early "wins" while the harder exercise habit catches up. A checklist is a self-monitoring tool, which is the single best-supported technique, especially when paired with a goal and feedback (which the checklist + progress tab already does).
- Habit stacking is really a form of implementation intention with a behavioural cue; the app can ask once during onboarding "when and where will you train?" and set reminders to that cue rather than to an arbitrary time.

### Gaps
- I did not find a trial specifically testing daily checklists in a fitness app on exercise adherence; evidence is by extension from self-monitoring BCT research.
- No study on "habit stacking" by that name in peer-reviewed exercise literature; the evidence is under implementation intentions and cue consistency.
- Lally's sample was young students; no replication in 30–55-year-old returners.

## Key Question 4: Streaks, challenges, all-or-nothing framing, lapse–relapse, and lessons from consumer apps

### Takeaway
Streaks motivate while intact but broken streaks measurably and durably suppress the behaviour; the best-evidenced fixes are (a) lowering what counts as "done" so streaks survive bad days (Duolingo), and (b) offering alternative ways to keep a streak alive (Silverman). The relapse-prevention literature's "abstinence violation effect" (guilt and catastrophising after a lapse) is the mechanism by which 75-Hard-style restart rules turn one missed day into quitting.

### Cited Findings
- Silverman & Barasch, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", Journal of Consumer Research 2023, using real language-app data (users who kept a streak, broke it, or lost it to a glitch): "broken streaks measurably suppress subsequent engagement, and the suppression is durable"; users with a broken streak were more likely to switch to a new language than rebuild in the same one. In an exercise scenario, 66.2% chose another strength exercise with an intact streak vs 57.9% with a broken one — [CU Boulder Leeds news](https://www.colorado.edu/business/news/2023/04/20/research-streaks-marketing-tech-barasch); [UDel](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- Silverman's design advice: rather than "Hey, you broke your streak!" notifications, "offer alternate ways to keep streaks going, making it easier while still promoting the behavior"; "what counts as a streak is malleable". Participants allowed to continue a streak via two different activity types were significantly more motivated than those restricted to one, with identical actual behaviour — [UDel](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)
- Silverman: three consecutive days of success produced more confidence in continuing than the same number of successes spread randomly across a week (streaks boost self-perceived commitment) — [UDel](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)
- "Streak creep": gamified streaks can backfire when the streak itself becomes the goal and loss is punishing — [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/fr-CA/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)
- Duolingo (company engineering blog): learners with a 7-day streak are 2.4× more likely to return the next day. Decoupling the streak from the full daily goal (one lesson now keeps the streak; goal progress shown separately) gave +3.3% 14-day retention, +1% DAU, +10.5% users on streaks within 20 days (+19% for new users); a year later just over half of daily learners had a 7+ day streak, up from about a third. Lesson: "lowering the barriers to building a consistent daily habit is more important ... than how much you learn each day, at least early on." — [Duolingo blog: Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- Duolingo reports users reaching a 10-day streak were less likely to churn and that it has run 600+ streak experiments; streak freezes exist specifically because "losing a streak can be very discouraging"; a "Streak Wager" experiment raised Day-7 retention by 14% — [Duolingo: how streaks keep learners committed](https://making.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals); [lazyweb research summary](https://lazyweb.com/research/duolingo-streak-goals-retention) (secondary)
- Peloton FY2025 10-K: average net monthly churn 1.6% (up from 1.4% in FY2024); members completing two or more disciplines per month had 60% lower churn; strength workouts reached 75% of the number of cycling workouts — [Peloton 10-K FY2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1639825/000163982525000138/pton-20250630.htm); [Q2 FY2025 shareholder letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1639825/000163982525000023/q22025shareholderletter.htm)
- Relapse Prevention Model applied to exercise: the model developed for addiction "has been successfully used to explore factors associated with exercise drop-out, or 'exercise relapse'"; one study tested whether mindfulness moderates the link between slip frequency and the Abstinence Violation Effect in lapsers — [Louisville dissertation](https://ir.library.louisville.edu/etd/1475); [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1646609)
- AVE described for clinical exercise: "the guilt, shame, or sense of failure a patient feels after a lapse — can turn a single missed week into a full relapse if the patient catastrophizes it" — [ACSM-CEP study guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/acsm-cep/education-behavior/adherence-relapse) (educational secondary source)
- 75 Hard: miss any task and "you go back to day one". Experts quoted in 2025–2026 press say its "rigid, all-or-nothing approach may undermine the long-term behavioral changes it promises", creates "perfectionism, a sense of failure and decreased motivation", and carries overtraining/burnout and disordered-eating risks — [CNN via ABC17](https://abc17news.com/cnn-health/2026/03/21/should-you-try-the-75-hard-challenge-experts-warn-the-risks-may-outweigh-the-benefits/); [Fox News health](https://www.foxnews.com/health/75-hard-challenge-promises-weight-loss-experts-warn-may-too-intense.amp); [Women's Health SA](https://womenshealthsa.co.za/?p=234398). No peer-reviewed completion-rate data for 75 Hard was found.
- Couch-to-5K interviews surfaced "negative emotions linked to non-completion" as a distinct theme, i.e. lapses carried an emotional cost that fed dropout — [IJERPH 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/20/17/6682)

### Inferences
- Onward's "no restart" rule is the correct response to the AVE and the broken-streak evidence. The remaining design choice is whether to show a streak at all: if shown, it must be a *flexible* streak (counts any completed day: workout, or a rest day with 3+ habits ticked, or a 10-minute "minimum" session) and must never reset to zero; "days completed out of days so far" and "weeks in a row with 2+ sessions" are safer counters than consecutive days.
- Duolingo's biggest retention win came from lowering the bar for "today counts". Onward's equivalent: let a day count as complete with a reduced session, and show full-session progress separately, rather than requiring every checklist item.
- Peloton's 60%-lower-churn for multi-discipline users supports mixing modalities (strength + bike/walk) rather than a single-mode programme.
- Copy after a missed day should be neutral and forward-looking ("Day 12 skipped. Day 13 is Strength B, 40 min") and never shaming; a shame-free re-entry is the single most important moment in the whole app.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found on streak design *in fitness apps specifically*; the evidence is from language-learning and consumer-research experiments.
- No completion-rate data for 75 Hard exists in the peer-reviewed literature.
- Strava and Peloton publish engagement correlations, not causal retention experiments.

## Key Question 5: Progress feedback that sustains motivation (overload visibility, PRs, weight trends, photos)

### Takeaway
Self-monitoring with feedback and goal review is the best-supported technique in the behaviour-change literature, and regular self-weighing improves weight outcomes without the psychological harm often assumed. The exercise literature frames progress feedback as "mastery experiences" that build self-efficacy, the strongest psychological predictor of adherence; evidence specific to PR badges or progress photos is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Michie 2009: self-monitoring + goal setting/feedback/goal review (control-theory cluster) 0.42 vs 0.26 effect size for interventions without it — [PubMed 19916637](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/)
- Self-weighing meta-analysis (2015): as a stand-alone strategy no significant effect (-0.5 kg, 95% CI -1.3 to 0.3), but adding self-weighing to a behavioural programme improves weight loss; trials with accountability showed greater loss (p = 0.03); no significant difference between daily and weekly weighing — [Is self-weighing an effective tool for weight loss, PMC4546162](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4546162/); [IJBNPA PDF](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12966-015-0267-4)
- Systematic review of self-weighing: regular self-weighing was associated with more weight loss "and not with adverse psychological outcomes (e.g., depression, anxiety)" — [Self-weighing in weight management, PubMed 25521523](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25521523/); [PMC2588640](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2588640/)
- Digital self-monitoring (diet, activity, weight): "greater adherence to self-monitoring of eating, physical activity, and weight each predicted greater weight loss"; app use frequency and number of self-weigh-ins at 8 weeks were important predictors of 4-month weight loss, with first-8-week app data explaining ~30% of 4-month variance — [Digital self-monitoring, PMC7156825](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7156825/); [JMIR mHealth 2019](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2019/2/e12209/PDF)
- Self-efficacy is described as "one of the biggest predictors of exercise adherence" and is built chiefly through mastery experiences; for new clients "the focus should be on exercise activities that they enjoy" and early positive experiences — [ACE Fitness (practitioner article)](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/6832/3-tips-to-help-clients-build-self-efficacy-for-exercise/) (secondary/practitioner source, grounded in Bandura)
- Teixeira 2012: competence need satisfaction and autonomous motivation predict exercise; intrinsic motivation (which competence feedback feeds) predicts long-term adherence — [Teixeira 2012](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78)
- Strava: in-app goal setting raised activity up to 15% — [road.cc](https://road.cc/content/news/254770-have-you-given-your-new-years-resolutions-yet)
- Noom RCT (427 adults, 16-week programme + 52-week follow-up, Obesity Science & Practice 2026): intervention lost 4.1% body weight vs control gaining 1.5%, with continued loss after the programme ended; separately, the most-engaged GLP-1 members lost 25.2% more weight than the least-engaged — [Noom press/research page](https://www.noom.com/in-the-news/noom-members-kept-losing-weight-a-full-year-after-the-program-ended-largest-ever-noom-randomized-clinical-trial-shows/); [Noom publications](https://www.noom.com/research/publications/) (company-reported; engagement–outcome links are correlational)

### Inferences
- Onward's existing "last lift" hints, body-weight chart with goal line, and per-habit bars are exactly the self-monitoring + feedback + goal cluster that Michie found most effective; the highest-value addition is lightweight *goal review* (e.g. a weekly summary that compares this week to last and lets the user adjust the goal).
- Strength PRs are a mastery experience; for a former athlete they also risk comparison with past bests. Frame PRs as "best since restart" / "Day 1 vs today" rather than lifetime records.
- Weekly or daily weigh-ins are both fine; what matters is that the chart shows a trend (rolling average) so single-day noise doesn't trigger the all-or-nothing reaction.
- Progress photos have little direct evidence either way; experts flag them as a potential trigger for disordered-eating-prone users (75 Hard coverage), so keep them optional and private.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study on PR notifications or progress photos and exercise adherence in healthy adults was found.
- Noom's evidence is company-reported and about diet behaviour change, not training.

## Key Question 6: Fat-loss-specific adherence (rate of loss, pairing with diet apps, avoiding overreach)

### Takeaway
Digital weight-loss programmes set and achieve modest, tailored rates (about 0.5–2 lb/week); the consistent predictor of success is frequency of dietary self-monitoring and self-weighing in the first ~8 weeks, not aggressiveness. Combining a training app with a separate diet app is reasonable because self-monitoring of eating, activity and weight each independently predicts loss, but every extra tracker raises the daily burden that drives the "lack of time" dropout.

### Cited Findings
- Digital interventions typically set tailored weight-loss goals of 0.5–2.0 lb/week — [JMIR mHealth 2019](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2019/2/e12209/PDF)
- "Consistent and frequent app-based dietary self-monitoring were associated with short-term weight loss"; dietary self-monitoring frequency was significantly associated with percent weight loss; median days of diet logging ranged 1.9–5.3 days/week across intervention types — [Adherence to mobile-app-based dietary self-monitoring (Duquesne)](https://dsc.duq.edu/faculty/321); [PMC9159560](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9159560); [PMC8319781](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8319781)
- Self-monitoring of eating, activity and weight each independently predicted weight loss; 8-week app data explained ~30% of 4-month loss — [PMC7156825](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7156825/)
- Self-weighing daily vs weekly showed no difference in outcome and no adverse psychological effects — [PMC4546162](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4546162/)
- In STRRIDE, dropouts had higher BMI and lower fitness, and 12% quit due to exacerbation of a prior injury — [PMC9165469](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9165469/)
- 75 Hard's combined diet + double-workout + no-alcohol rules draw expert warnings about overtraining, burnout and disordered eating — [CNN via ABC17](https://abc17news.com/cnn-health/2026/03/21/should-you-try-the-75-hard-challenge-experts-warn-the-risks-may-outweigh-the-benefits/)
- Noom's 2023 analysis: 75% of participants maintained at least 5% weight loss at 1 year and 49% maintained 10% — [Noom in the news](https://www.noom.com/in-the-news/people-who-use-noom-the-most-also-lose-the-most-weight/) (company-reported)

### Inferences
- For the founder's ~15–20 lb goal, a 0.5–1 lb/week trend over 75 days (roughly 6–11 lb) is the realistic in-programme outcome; the app should show a goal line that reflects that rate rather than the full goal by Day 75, to avoid the goal being "failed" on schedule.
- Keeping diet tracking in a dedicated app (e.g. RP Diet Coach) and only mirroring a single daily "protein" tick in Onward respects the evidence (self-monitoring diet works) without turning Onward into a second logging chore; the key is that the daily checklist stays at five items.
- Because higher-BMI and previously injured participants drop out more, the comeback programme's conservative ramp and swap system double as fat-loss adherence features, not just safety features.

### Gaps
- No studies were found specifically on pairing a strength-training app with a separate diet app; the inference is from independent predictors.
- No peer-reviewed RP Diet Coach outcome data was found.
- No evidence on an "ideal" caloric deficit for adherence in returning athletes specifically; the 0.5–2 lb/week range comes from general digital weight-loss programmes.
