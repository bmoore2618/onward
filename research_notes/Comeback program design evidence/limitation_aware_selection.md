# Limitation-aware exercise selection and substitution (low back, knee, shoulder)

Scope: evidence and practitioner consensus a product team can use to build "limitation toggles" (swap default movements, add cues) in a general-fitness app. Non-clinical framing throughout. Sources are labelled as (a) guideline/systematic review, (b) primary study, (c) practitioner source, or (d) product/legal document. Research date: 6 Oct 2026. Several primary pages (Cochrane Library, PubMed, JOSPT PDF, DOAJ) returned 403/blocked for fetching; where that happened the abstract was taken from a secondary indexing record (PEDro) or the search snippet, and this is noted.

## Key question 1: Low back (history of pain, disc issues, fusion) — what the evidence says about resistance training, which patterns are commonly modified, and common substitutions

### Takeaway
Current guideline-level evidence treats exercise, including resistance/strength training, as a first-line, effective and low-harm option for chronic non-specific low back pain, and no exercise mode is a clear loser; the "safe vs. dangerous movement" debate (spinal flexion, heavy axial load) is a practitioner-level disagreement with weak direct evidence, so a product should frame swaps as comfort/tolerance preferences rather than safety rules. After lumbar fusion, the literature supports progressive exercise once the surgeon has cleared the patient (typically 3–6+ months), and strength coaches with clinical backgrounds report training fused patients through squats and deadlifts with conservative, tolerance-based loading, but formal long-term "what to avoid" evidence is thin and surgeon advice varies.

### Cited Findings

Guideline / systematic-review level
- Cochrane 2021 (Hayden et al.), 249 RCTs, 24,486 participants: exercise is effective for chronic non-specific LBP compared to usual care, no care or placebo; described as one of the largest reviews in the Cochrane Library — [Cochrane Library record](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009790.pub2/information)
- Same review, abstract via PEDro: moderate-certainty evidence for pain at earliest follow-up, MD −15.2 on 0–100 (95% CI −18.3 to −12.2) vs no treatment/usual care/placebo; moderate-certainty for functional limitation, MD −6.8 (95% CI −8.3 to −5.3), which did not meet the authors' prespecified minimal clinically important difference. Adverse effects were reported in 37 of 112 exercise groups (33%) vs 12 of 42 comparison groups (29%); where measured systematically, median 0.14 events per participant in exercise groups (mostly minor, e.g. muscle soreness) vs 0.12 in comparison groups — [PEDro record of Hayden 2021](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/66912)
- A 2024 Cochrane-collaborative protocol notes roughly 200 new eligible RCTs have been published since the 2021 review (which included trials up to 2018) and that "general strengthening exercise" (load-bearing/resistance exercise targeting at least two body areas) was used in 49 of the 249 trials; a dedicated review of general strengthening exercise for chronic LBP is in progress, so results are not yet available — [PMC protocol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10929355)
- Owen et al. 2020, BJSM network meta-analysis (89 studies, 5,578 patients): Pilates had the highest probability of being best for pain (SUCRA 100%; SMD −1.86, 95% CI −2.54 to −1.19); resistance training had the highest probability for physical function (SUCRA 80%; SMD −1.14, −1.71 to −0.56) and mental health (SUCRA 80%; −1.26, −2.10 to −0.41); stabilisation/motor control was similar for function (SUCRA 80%; −1.13, −1.53 to −0.74) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31666220/); [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/21/1279)
- Posterior-chain resistance training (deadlift-type, hip-hinge) vs general exercise/walking for chronic LBP: systematic review and meta-analysis exists (PMC7940464) evaluating PCRT in sedentary/recreationally active people with CLBP — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7940464/). (Search snippet only; effect sizes not verified from full text.)
- Resistance training for subacute/persistent low-back-related leg pain: 2024 systematic review on effectiveness and optimal dose — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10981532/). (Snippet only; conclusions not verified.)
- A systematic review of resistance training in musculoskeletal rehabilitation (BJSM) concluded RT can increase strength, reduce pain and improve function in chronic LBP — [search-surfaced record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4027422?lng=en). (Snippet only.)
- Lancet Low Back Pain Series 2018 (Foster et al.): recommends a biopsychosocial framework with initial non-pharmacological treatment, education supporting self-management and "resumption of normal activities and exercise"; notes global gaps including inappropriately high use of imaging, rest, opioids, injections and surgery — [VU research record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/prevention-and-treatment-of-low-back-pain-evidence-challenges-and/); [summary](https://atlas.chiro.org/?p=25871)
- NICE NG59: recommends considering a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body or combined) for a specific episode or flare-up of LBP with or without sciatica, choosing exercise type according to the person's needs, preferences and capabilities; the July 2026 update left the imaging, self-management and group-exercise recommendations unchanged but withdrew recommendations 1.2.13 (psychological therapy) and 1.2.14 (combined physical and psychological programmes) — [NICE NG59 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendation); [Physitrack summary of 2026 update](https://www.physitrack.com/insights/nice-low-back-pain-guideline-update-2026)
- JOSPT 2021 clinical practice guideline (George et al., "Interventions for the Management of Acute and Chronic Low Back Pain: Revision 2021") provides graded (A/B/C) recommendations for exercise training across acute LBP, acute LBP with leg pain, and chronic LBP — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34719942/); [PMC summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10508241). (Full text fetch blocked; exact grades per exercise type not verified, see Gaps.)
- RESTORE trial (Lancet, June 2023; Kent, Haines, O'Sullivan et al.): Cognitive Functional Therapy, an individualised approach targeting unhelpful pain-related cognitions, emotions and behaviours, delivered in 20 Australian physio clinics; more than 80% of participants satisfied; 3-year follow-up (2025) showed sustained, clinically significant improvements in pain-related activity limitation and pain intensity vs usual care after a seven-session programme — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37146623/); [Curtin University news](https://www.curtin.edu.au/research/news/fresh-hope-for-australians-living-with-chronic-back-pain?type=media); [Macquarie 3-year follow-up](https://lighthouse.mq.edu.au/article/august-2025/breakthrough-back-pain-treatment-still-working-after-three-years-new-data)

The flexion / "spine hygiene" debate (practitioner sources, clearly labelled)
- McGill position (practitioner/biomechanics): lumbar flexion under load "exponentially increases shearing stress on the spine" and reduces the load needed to injure a disc; neutral-spine lifting is the most widely recommended technique in research; Suni et al. 2013 RCT found back-movement training including avoiding full lumbar flexion reduced LBP in military personnel. The same article concedes: "We don't have any studies that compare lifters squatting or deadlifting appreciable weights with a neutral vs. flexed spine and measuring how often they get injured" — [Menno Henselmans summary](https://mennohenselmans.com/is-spinal-flexion-dangerous/)
- McGill "Big 3" (curl-up, side plank, bird dog) were selected via EMG and spinal-load testing as the exercises that best activated spinal stabilisers while minimising compression and shear — [EliteFTS](https://elitefts.com/education/correct-application-and-execution-for-the-mcgill-big-3)
- Counter-position (Greg Lehman, "movement optimism"): challenges flexion as inherently harmful; suggests varying spine postures across tasks (lift, bend, squat, row) to balance stress and recovery; "movement optimism" = humans are resilient and adaptable given the right stimuli — [Physio Network](https://www.physio-network.com/?p=1578); [podcast listing](https://music.amazon.ca/podcasts/c8bf41a3-d178-486c-bfee-7225783d638a/episodes/47aa44df-6e1e-4e1b-9d7c-2d60f4b1a30f/new-directions-podcast-growth-and-advocacy-in-physiotherapy-episode-3-what-is-movement-optimism-and-how-can-it-fit-in-physiotherapy-with-greg-lehman)
- Physiotutors review: early animal/cadaver work (Wade 2014/2017; Berger-Roscher 2017) showed higher injury potential loading a segment in flexion vs neutral, but living tissue adapts; Mawston 2021 found flexed-spine lifting produced higher trunk-extensor moments with lower EMG (more efficient); acknowledges "lumbar flexion is often painful in patients with low back pain" while arguing avoiding it entirely is neither possible nor efficient — [Physiotutors](https://www.physiotutors.com/lifting-with-a-flexed-lumbar-spine-safe-and-possibly-even-beneficial/)
- Stronger By Science has a dedicated lumbar-flexion evidence review — [strongerbyscience.com/lumbar-flexion](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/lumbar-flexion/). (Not fetched; listed for the writer.)
- Barbell Medicine (physician-led coaching): 12-week Low Back Pain Rehab template uses progressive loading, movement modification and three phases (symptom management → normalisation → return to training), RPE-based load management, 3 resistance + 2 conditioning sessions/week, with flexible exercise selection "to match your tolerance, goals, and equipment" — [Barbell Medicine template](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/shop/training-templates/rehab/low-back-pain-rehab-template-2/); evidence-based LBP guide — [Barbell Medicine article](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/articles/articles-injury-rehab/)
- A prospective case series of barbell strength training for non-specific LBP reported significant reduction in pain-related restriction of daily life and increased trunk extension/flexion force output — [record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4075849?lng=en). (Snippet only.)

After lumbar fusion
- Systematic reviews: Greenwood et al. 2016 (rehabilitation after lumbar fusion) note clinical outcomes lag surgical outcomes and 40% of patients have significant back-related disability 12 months post-fusion — [Kingston record](https://researchinnovation.kingston.ac.uk/en/publications/rehabilitation-following-lumbar-fusion-surgery-a-systematic-revie-2/). A separate review found evidence generally supports formal rehabilitation after fusion and that starting PT at 12 weeks post-op gave better outcomes at lower cost than a 6-week start — [scoliosis.org summary](https://www.scoliosis.org/the-role-of-physical-therapy-and-rehabilitation-after-lumbar-fusion-surgery-for-degenerative-disease-a-systematic-review/). A meta-analysis of exercise after fusion (8 studies, 3 meta-analysed) found cognitive therapy/consultation plus exercise outperformed exercise alone, and core-stabilisation gains in strength/endurance were short-term only — [MU record](https://acikerisim.mu.edu.tr/xmlui/handle/20.500.12809/10236)
- Surgeon-authored patient guidance (spine-health, Dr John Sherman, orthopaedic spine surgeon, peer-reviewed page): a controlled progressive exercise programme typically starts 6 weeks–3 months post-op; excess bending, lifting and twisting avoided during the first ~3 months; once imaging confirms solid fusion, "a full return to an active lifestyle—including bending, lifting, and twisting—is permitted," typically ~6 months, sometimes closer to 12; complete recovery up to 8 months with bone evolving 12–18 months; long-term advice is to keep stretching/strengthening the back, aerobic exercise and bending at the knees for heavy items — [Spine-health](https://www.spine-health.com/treatment/spinal-fusion/spinal-fusion-surgery-recovery-3-months-and-after)
- Contrasting (commercial clinic) guidance claims "permanent restrictions" such as a 20–40 lb lifting limit, no high-impact exercise/contact sports and avoiding repetitive bending/twisting — [Premia Spine](https://premiaspine.com/?p=8878). This is a marketing site for a fusion alternative; treat as low-quality and note that it conflicts with the surgeon-authored page above.
- Barbell Medicine forum, Dr Austin Baraki (physician) on training after a healed fusion: "We have trained many individuals post-fusion to perform those lifts" (leg press, deadlift); "Start conservatively and load gradually based on tolerance, and avoid pairing your coaching with unnecessary messages of danger around exercise with respect to her back" — [Barbell Medicine forum](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/exercising-post-fully-healed-lumbar-fusion/7681)
- A 2022 return-to-sport-after-lumbar-fusion paper (degenerative spinal disease) exists in the Journal of Education, Health and Sport — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/f41fd887a0fa40b6ba3249e66f8f063d). (Fetch blocked; figures not verified.)

Commonly modified patterns and substitutions (practitioner consensus; no RCT compares substitution menus)
- Barbell Physio (Zach Long, DPT) and Barbell Medicine both emphasise finding tolerable variations and keeping people lifting rather than removing patterns — [Barbell Physio](https://thebarbellphysio.com/training-shoulder-pain/); [Barbell Medicine template](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/shop/training-templates/rehab/low-back-pain-rehab-template-2/)
- The specific swap list requested (goblet/box squats, chest-supported rows, glute bridges, split squats, loaded carries in place of heavy axial loading, loaded flexion, ballistic hinges and high-impact plyometrics) was not found in a single citable guideline; it reflects common practice in the practitioner sources above and in Fitbod's product behaviour (see Q5), and the writer should present it as "commonly used by coaches/physios" rather than evidence-graded.

### Inferences
- The guideline-level message for product copy is: moving and loading is good, type matters less than doing it consistently, and harms are mostly minor soreness. This supports a "preference" framing ("choose what feels good today") over a "danger" framing.
- Because flexion/axial-load danger is contested and the strongest human evidence is absent, swaps for the low-back toggle are best justified as comfort, confidence and tolerance choices (e.g. "front-loaded squats and supported rows tend to feel better for many people with a back history"), with the option to return to the default movement when the user wants.
- For post-fusion users the only consistent facts are: (1) surgeon clearance gates return to unrestricted activity, (2) progression should be gradual and tolerance-based, (3) long-term formal restrictions vary by surgeon. A product should therefore ask "Has your doctor cleared you for unrestricted exercise?" rather than hard-code restrictions, and default to lower-impact, supported variants while leaving the user in control.
- Barbell Medicine's and Hinge Health's use of three phases and "as tolerated" progression maps naturally to an app that starts conservative and adds load/range as the user reports comfort.

### Gaps
- Exact JOSPT 2021 grades per exercise type (A/B/C) could not be verified because the PDF and PubMed were blocked.
- No RCT or systematic review directly compares "modified" vs "unmodified" strength programmes for people with a back history in a general-fitness setting; the swap list is consensus, not evidence.
- No reliable long-term data were found on heavy barbell training or plyometrics after single-level fusion (adjacent-segment risk with lifting); surgeon advice conflicts (full return vs permanent 20–40 lb limits).
- Posterior-chain RT meta-analysis effect sizes and the 2024 low-back-related leg-pain RT review conclusions were not verified from full text.

## Key question 2: Knees (patellofemoral and meniscal history) — common swaps and evidence quality

### Takeaway
Guidelines strongly support strength and aerobic exercise for knee OA and degenerative meniscal tears, and exercise matches surgery at 10 years for degenerative meniscus; for patellofemoral pain, combined hip-and-knee exercise is the core recommendation. Specific swaps (reduced-depth or box squats, step-ups, reverse lunges, cycling) are grounded in biomechanical studies of patellofemoral joint stress and practitioner consensus rather than outcome trials.

### Cited Findings
- JOSPT 2019 Patellofemoral Pain CPG (APTA Orthopaedic Section): clinicians should include exercise therapy with combined hip- and knee-targeted exercises to reduce pain and improve function short, medium and long term; exercise is "the critical component"; biophysical agents (ultrasound, cryotherapy, e-stim, laser) and EMG/visual biofeedback are not recommended; optional adjuncts include foot orthoses, taping, patellar mobilisation and gait retraining for runners — [Physical Therapy First summary](https://physicaltherapyfirst.com/blog/patellofemoral-pain-clinical-practice-guidelines/); [AAFP summary PDF](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2020/1001/p442.pdf)
- OARSI 2019 guidelines strongly recommend exercise (land-based aerobic, strength, mind-body) for knee OA; strength training, self-management/education and weight management are core for all individuals — [APTA summary of OARSI](https://apta.org/patient-care/evidence-based-practice-resources/cpgs/oarsi-guidelines-for-the-non-surgical-management-of-knee-hip-and-polyarticular-osteoarthritis); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8207290)
- OMEX RCT 10-year follow-up (Berg et al. 2025, BJSM): 140 participants aged 35–60 with degenerative medial meniscal tears; arthroscopic partial meniscectomy vs exercise therapy showed no significant difference in radiographic OA progression (OARSI scores 1.81 vs 1.42), substantial KOOS improvement in both arms, no meaningful strength difference; "exercise is at least as effective as APM" — [Physiotutors research summary](https://www.physiotutors.com/research/meniscectomy-or-exercise-for-degenerative-meniscus/)
- A consensus-based process (TeMPO trial) identified physical therapy and home-exercise treatments for degenerative meniscal tear with knee OA, noting few programmes were designed for that population — [University of Melbourne record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1421708-a-consensus-based-process-identifying-physical-therapy-and-exercise-treatments-for-patients-with-degenerative-meniscal-tears-and-knee-oa--the-tempo-physical-therapy-interventions-and-home-exercise-program)
- Squat depth biomechanics (female lifters, back squat): patellofemoral joint reaction force increased with load and with shallower depth, while PFJ stress increased with load and with depth; the authors recommend either full-depth squats or loaded squats to less than 90° of knee flexion to minimise PFJ stress — [Montana State thesis](https://scholarworks.montana.edu/items/e78fed7a-e8e3-4bcc-ab6f-b40a90230fff/full). An older study found peak PFJ force/stress did not differ between 70°, 90° and 110° in collegiate women — [PubMed 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11390050/). These conflict; the writer should state depth findings are mixed.
- Lunge biomechanics: forward lunges produced greater PFJ stress at low knee angles (10–30°) and side lunges at 40–100°; at 60° descent and 40–60° ascent, PFJ force and stress were greater lunging at ground level than onto a 10 cm platform — [IJSPT article](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/31876.pdf)
- Step-ups (forward, lateral, step-down) have been studied with step height set for 45° knee flexion, i.e. a controllable-range exercise — [search-surfaced study](https://repository.li.mahidol.ac.th/handle/123456789/10556). (Snippet only.)
- Fitbod's product behaviour for a "Knee" limitation excludes exercises that could stress the area and recommends alternatives more often (see Q5 for wording) — [Fitbod help](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/37629269518103)

### Inferences
- Evidence quality: strong for "keep strengthening hips and quads" (guidelines, OMEX), moderate-to-weak for which specific variant to pick (biomechanics studies, no outcome trials). Product copy should say "many people with cranky knees prefer…" rather than "this protects your knee."
- Biomechanics supports controllable-range options (box squat to a chosen height, step-ups with adjustable box, reverse lunge or lunge to a platform) and reducing load before reducing depth, which is a clean rule for an app: first lighten, then shorten range, then swap.
- Cycling (the founder has a Peloton) is a standard low-impact conditioning swap for knee limitations in practice, but no specific citation was collected for it.

### Gaps
- No citation found specifically endorsing "avoid deep plyometrics" for knee history; this is practice-based.
- No head-to-head trial of box squat vs full squat, or reverse vs forward lunge, on knee pain outcomes.
- Cycling-as-substitute evidence not searched in depth.

## Key question 3: Shoulders — common swaps and evidence quality

### Takeaway
For rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain the evidence supports progressive, graded loading (at least equal to surgery for several diagnoses), but roughly 30% don't respond, and exercise mode comparisons are weak. Swaps toward neutral-grip, landmine/incline angles, floor press and a row emphasis are practitioner consensus (sports physios), not trial-proven.

### Cited Findings
- Rotator cuff-related shoulder pain (RCRSP) is the most common shoulder condition; exercise is the main non-surgical intervention; for ~30% of people it does not significantly reduce symptoms, possibly due to inappropriate dosage or choice of exercises (Lewis et al. BMJ Open protocol) — [DOAJ/BMJ Open](https://doaj.org/article/0030f1f268a345ba8e996e0a4b86b24a); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7646354)
- A graduated, well-constructed exercise approach provides at least equivalent benefit to surgery for subacromial pain syndrome, rotator cuff tendinopathy, partial-thickness and atraumatic full-thickness tears (Lewis, Canadian Physiotherapy Association course description) — [CPA/Embodia](https://cpa.embodiaapp.com/courses/1721-rotator-cuff-related-shoulder-pain-an-update-canadian-physiotherapy-association-orthopaedic-division-dr-jeremy-lewis)
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of specific exercise modes for RCRSP (2025): scapular stabilisation improved pain and function; mobilisation/stretching improved function and pain; eccentric exercise improved performance outcomes — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011739/)
- Barbell Physio (Zach Long, PT, DPT, SCS): "Rows very seldom irritate shoulder pain but very often are neglected"; the landmine press "often lets athletes train above 90 degrees of shoulder elevation without pain"; also recommends bottoms-up kettlebell carries, push-up variations and triceps work during shoulder-pain periods; approach is to keep training with modified movements — [Barbell Physio](https://thebarbellphysio.com/training-shoulder-pain/)
- Barbell Physio on overhead-press variations while injured: neutral grip (palms facing) reduces stress on the shoulder and shifts load to different tissues; half-kneeling landmine press works in a diagonal plane, limits range and avoids the overhead lockout; as tolerance improves "gradually lean further into the press to increase the range of motion" — [Barbell Physio](https://thebarbellphysio.com/?p=39493); [Sole summary](https://www.soletreadmills.com/blogs/news/top-3-overhead-press-alternatives-for-shoulder-pain)

### Inferences
- Shoulder toggle rules supported by the above: default to row-heavy pulling, neutral-grip dumbbell or landmine pressing, floor/incline press before flat barbell bench, and progress range as comfort allows. Evidence quality: clinical guidance for loading is good; specific variant choice is expert opinion.
- The founder's garage has a landmine attachment and dumbbells, so these swaps are implementable without new equipment.

### Gaps
- No trial compares neutral-grip vs pronated pressing, or landmine vs overhead press, on shoulder-pain outcomes.
- "Floor press" specifically was not found in a citable source; it is common gym practice.

## Key question 4: Progression and effort for people with limitations — RPE/RIR evidence and "stop/substitute" rules

### Takeaway
RPE/RIR-based (autoregulated) loading is at least as effective as percentage-based loading for strength and is the standard tool used in rehab-leaning coaching templates, but RIR estimates are less accurate in untrained people and in high-rep sets, so an app should pair RIR targets with conservative defaults and simple stop rules. Clinical "stop and get checked" rules centre on new neurological symptoms (bilateral leg pain/weakness, saddle numbness, bladder/bowel changes) and on severe pain or loss of function during or after exercise.

### Cited Findings
- Review of subjective/objective autoregulation (2021): autoregulation groups (Helms et al.; Graham & Cleather) achieved greater 1RM increases in squat and bench than percentage-based groups, partly because "the autoregulation groups trained at significantly higher intensities than the control groups, even if the intensity was assigned to theoretically be the same"; RPE/RIR is useful without devices; however "RIR has been reported to be less accurate among untrained subjects" and in higher-rep sets, and authors suggest it may be "better for prescribing training intensity among experienced resistance-trained participants during sets with a relatively low number of repetitions" — [PMC7810043](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7810043/)
- Network meta-analysis of autoregulated RT for maximal strength: APRE, velocity-based and RPE-based methods were significantly more effective than percentage-based training, APRE largest effect — [record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4094556). (Snippet only.)
- 2025 systematic review (Russo et al.) on RIR scale accuracy: accuracy is greater close to failure, in upper-limb exercises, and decreases as reps increase and relative load decreases — [University of Évora PDF](https://dspace.uevora.pt/rdpc/bitstream/10174/39438/1/2025%20Russo%20et%20al.%20Factors%20influencing%20the%20accuracy%20of%20the%20repetition%20in%20reserve%20scale%20in%20resistance%20training%20%20a%20systematic%20review.pdf)
- "Autoregulation in Resistance Training: Addressing the Inconsistencies" (2020) defines autoregulation as adjusting training based on measured performance or perceived capability and notes inconsistent terminology limits synthesis — [PMC7575491](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7575491/)
- Barbell Medicine's LBP rehab template uses RPE-based load and intensity management across three phases — [Barbell Medicine](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/shop/training-templates/rehab/low-back-pain-rehab-template-2/)
- Cauda equina red flags (NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde patient leaflet): saddle/perineal numbness, urinary retention or incontinence, loss of sensation when passing urine, faecal incontinence; if any appear since back/leg pain started or worsened, go to A&E as soon as possible — [NHSGGC leaflet](https://rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/media/301h1exb/cauda-equina-syndrome-important-warning-signs-nhsggc.pdf). Pain in both legs with weakness is described as the closest thing to a reliable independent predictor — [PainScience](https://www.painscience.com/blog/updated-red-flag-for-a-rare-type-of-back-pain.html). Outcomes are better with surgery within 48 hours of acute symptom onset — [MMU paper](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/621393/3/Assessment%20and%20Management%20of%20Cauda%20Equina%20Syndrome.pdf)
- Product-level stop rules in use: Hinge Health — "If you experience severe pain or loss of function in any body area during or after the use of the Hinge Health Programme, stop the exercise immediately"; "If you feel insecure in your ability to execute or recover from the recommended body position for a movement… do not perform that exercise" — [Hinge Health user manual](https://www.hingehealth.com/user-manual/). Kaia — "If you experience severe pain or loss of function in any body part during or after the use of Kaia Back Pain, stop the exercise immediately and consult a physician" — [Kaia instructions for use](https://kaiahealth.com/legal/instructions-for-use)

### Inferences
- For a comeback population (detrained, often novice-like perception of effort), an app should (1) prescribe RIR targets of ~2–4 rather than 0–1, (2) use lower-rep sets when the user estimates RIR, and (3) start loads from a conservative anchor (last lift or bodyweight) so that misjudged RIR errs light. These are inferences from the accuracy findings above, not from a trial in this population.
- A simple, non-diagnostic stop rule consistent with the clinical and product sources: "Stop and swap if an exercise causes sharp or spreading pain, numbness, tingling or weakness in an arm or leg. Stop and see a doctor urgently for numbness around the groin/saddle area or changes in bladder or bowel control." The second sentence mirrors NHS patient leaflets and is appropriate general safety information, not diagnosis.

### Gaps
- No study of RPE/RIR prescription specifically in people with a back/knee/shoulder history in a general-fitness setting.
- No citation found for the common coaching phrase "pain up to 3/10 that settles by the next day is acceptable"; it is widely used in tendinopathy rehab but was not retrieved here.

## Key question 5: Product and legal framing — how apps describe modifications and disclaimers; App Store review guidance

### Takeaway
Consumer fitness apps (Peloton, Fitbod) frame limitation features as "work around" or "adjust" and pair them with "not medical advice / consult a professional" language; regulated digital-therapeutic apps (Hinge Health, Kaia) carry formal contraindication lists, "doctor's instructions take precedence" clauses, and explicit stop rules because they are medical devices. Apple's guideline 1.4.1 applies extra scrutiny to apps that could diagnose or treat and expects reminders to check with a doctor; 5.1.3 restricts use of health/fitness data.

### Cited Findings
- Fitbod (consumer fitness): "Injury exclusions" feature — "helps you work around injuries or limitations by adjusting your workout plan"; users add an injury via My Plan → Injuries/Limitations by typing a description or choosing quick options such as Lower Back, Shoulder or Knee; "exercises that could stress the affected area are excluded, and alternative exercises that work around your condition are recommended more often" (e.g. wrist → push-ups excluded, neutral-wrist machine/cable presses favoured); disclaimer: suggestions "are not medical advice and cannot replace professional guidance… always consult a qualified healthcare professional before exercising with an injury" — [Fitbod help centre](https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/37629269518103). ### Inferences
- The consumer-app pattern (Fitbod, Peloton) is the right template for Onward: name the feature as a training preference ("Work around: lower back / knee / shoulder"), describe it as adjusting exercise selection, and attach a short "not medical advice; check with your doctor if you have an injury or were told to limit activity" line in onboarding/settings. Avoid Hinge/Kaia-style contraindication lists, which signal a medical device.
- For App Review, keep copy about fitness and habits ("feels better," "easier on," "many people prefer") and avoid "protects," "rehabs," "treats," "safe for your spine"; include a doctor reminder somewhere visible; when HealthKit arrives, disclose exactly which data types are read and never use them for marketing.
- Post-fusion users: a simple self-declared "cleared by my doctor for unrestricted exercise" toggle (as Peloton's affirmation does) keeps responsibility with the user and the clinician.
