# Resistance-training program structure and progression for deconditioned adults (~30–55) returning after a layoff: 10–12 weeks, 3 sessions/week

Scope note: Population is healthy, previously active but now deconditioned adults; not athletes, not clinical rehab. Most meta-analytic evidence below pools untrained and trained participants; where a study is specifically about untrained/novice or detrained-retraining people this is flagged. Practitioner sources (Stronger By Science, Renaissance Periodization, NSCA PTQ) are labelled as such. Older landmark papers (pre-2015) are labelled "landmark".

---

## Key Question 1: Full-body vs. upper/lower vs. body-part splits at 3 sessions/week (hypertrophy, strength, adherence, volume equated)

### Takeaway
When weekly set volume is equated, training a muscle 1×, 2× or 3×+ per week produces statistically indistinguishable hypertrophy; for strength, higher per-muscle frequency shows a small advantage in some analyses (mainly multi-joint upper-body lifts) and none in others. At 3 sessions/week, a full-body layout (each pattern hit ~3×/week) or an upper/lower/full hybrid are both evidence-consistent; the deciding factors are practical (session length, movement practice, missed-day tolerance), not physiology. Direct adherence comparisons between splits are essentially absent from the literature.

### Cited Findings
- Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger (2019), 25 studies: "resistance training frequency does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated"; a significant effect favoring higher frequency appeared only when volume was not equated, and even then the 1 vs 3+ day difference was "modest" — [Schoenfeld 2019 summary (Henselmans)](https://mennohenselmans.com/?p=8631); [record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4052540?lng=en)
- Earlier Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2016), 10 studies: higher frequency ES 0.49 ± 0.08 vs lower 0.30 ± 0.07 (P = 0.002); "frequencies of training twice a week promote superior hypertrophic outcomes to once a week." Note this 2016 analysis was largely superseded by the 2019 volume-equated analysis — [Schoenfeld 2016, Sports Med (PubMed 27102172)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27102172/)
- Grgic et al. (2018) strength meta-analysis, 22 studies: effect sizes rose with frequency (0.74, 0.82, 0.93, 1.08 for 1, 2, 3, 4+ days/week), but in volume-equated studies there was no significant frequency effect (p = 0.421). Frequency mattered for multi-joint exercises (p < 0.001) but not single-joint (p = 0.324); for upper body (p = 0.004) but not lower body (p = 0.070); for young adults (p = 0.024) but not older adults (p = 0.093). Authors note low frequency "may be an appropriate intersession frequency dose to produce strength gains for untrained or older individuals" — [Grgic 2018, Sports Med](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9); [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5684266/)
- Pelland et al. (SportRxiv preprint; 67 studies, 2,058 participants): frequency had a negligible relationship with hypertrophy once volume was accounted for, but strength "gains increase with increasing frequency, albeit with diminishing returns" (posterior probability 100%). Introduced "fractional" set counting (indirect sets = 0.5) — [Pelland preprint, SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); secondary summary — [Biolayne REPS](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-31/the-king-of-volume-metas/)
- RCT in untrained women (Pedersen et al. 2022, n = 50): full-body 2×/week vs upper/lower split 4×/week, same exercises and weekly sets/reps — "No between-group differences were found in any of the variables" — [Pedersen 2022, BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil](https://bmcsportsscimedrehabil.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13102-022-00481-7)
- RCT in untrained adults (Evangelista et al. 2021, n = 67): A/B split 2×/week (8 sets/session) vs full-body 4×/week, equal weekly sets — "Resistance training twice or four times per week has similar effects on neuromuscular adaptation, provided weekly set volume is equal" — [Einstein (São Paulo) 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8372753/)
- Trained men, 3 vs 6 sessions/week with volume equated: similar strength and hypertrophy — [Grgic et al. 2018 (ResearchGate PDF)](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932_Resistance_Training_Frequencies_of_3_and_6_Times_Per_Week_Produce_Similar_Muscular_Adaptations_in_Resistance-Trained_Men/links/5bd26d6892851c6b278f311d/Resistance-Training-Frequencies-of-3-and-6-Times-Per-Week-Produce-Similar-Muscular-Adaptations-in-Resistance-Trained-Men.pdf)
- Iversen et al. (2021) narrative review: "training a muscle 1 day per week appears to induce similar strength gains as training ≥ 3 times per week if the total training volume is the same" — [Iversen 2021, Sports Med (PMC8449772)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)
- Practitioner (Stronger By Science, Nuckols): own meta-analysis of 13 volume-equated studies found higher frequency gave roughly 20–23% faster strength gains overall, driven by upper-body pressing (3+ sessions/week better; "up to at least 4"), with minimal frequency effect for squats. Advises that when adding a session, "dialing back your per-session volume until you adjust (keeping your weekly volume unchanged)" and making the extra session an "easy" session 3–4 reps from failure — [Stronger By Science: Training Frequency](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/training-frequency/)
- Practitioner (Stronger By Science, summarized): beginners (< ~6 months) do well with 2 full-body days/week, with 4 days as another option; train each muscle about twice weekly — [summary via thefitness.wiki/SBS](https://thefitness.wiki/faq/should-i-train-full-body-or-a-body-part-split/)
- Practitioner (NSCA PTQ): frequent practice of fundamental patterns helps novices build movement competency; "It is the personal trainer's task to help novice clients build proficiency in resistance training movement patterns" — [NSCA PTQ 10.2.1](https://dxpprod.nsca.com/contentassets/3d09f06f0b4c4f6fbd8cc382ed1f3d4a/ptq-10.2.1-progressive-strategies-for-teaching-fundamental-resistance-training-movement-patterns.pdf)
- Landmark (ACSM 2009 position stand): novice frequency recommendation is 2–3 days/week, whole-body — [ACSM Position Stand 2009 (PDF)](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/ACSM-Position-Stand-Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a 3-day/week returner, a full-body template (or an A/B alternating full-body) is the most defensible default: each pattern gets 2–3 exposures per week, which (a) satisfies the "≥2×/week" heuristic, (b) gives more frequent technique practice, and (c) is robust to a missed day — a skipped session removes a third of one week's volume for every muscle rather than all of one body region's volume. This is a design inference; no trial has directly tested missed-day robustness.
- Upper/lower splits need 4 days to hit each region 2×/week; at 3 days they produce uneven weekly frequency (e.g., 2 upper / 1 lower one week). Body-part splits at 3 days give 1×/week per muscle, which is equivalent for hypertrophy if volume is equal but makes each session long and places all of a muscle's weekly volume in one bout — a worse fit for someone whose recovery capacity is low.
- Higher per-muscle frequency may give a small strength edge for upper-body pressing; this is a tie-breaker, not a driver.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares adherence, enjoyment or dropout between full-body and split routines in untrained or returning adults; the two untrained RCTs above measured only strength/size. Adherence is a design assumption, not a measured outcome.
- No study specifically on "formerly trained, long-detrained 30–55-year-olds" comparing splits.

## Key Question 2: Minimum effective dose and volume landmarks for novices/returners (sets/week, RIR/RPE, rep ranges)

### Takeaway
About 4 weekly sets per muscle group is a defensible floor (Iversen 2021), with ~10 sets/week giving meaningfully more hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2017) and diminishing returns thereafter; in true novices even single sets 3×/week produce measurable gains. Loads of 6–15 RM (roughly 60–85% 1RM) with sets stopped ~1–3 reps short of failure are adequate for both strength and size; training to failure is not required and may slightly impair dynamic strength gains.

### Cited Findings
Volume
- Iversen et al. (2021): recommends "performing a minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group," with "≥ 10 sets" weekly if time permits; for untrained individuals "performing only a single set three times per week is effective for increasing strength and hypertrophy" — [Iversen 2021 (PMC8449772)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2017), 15 studies / 34 groups: each additional weekly set associated with ES +0.023 (~+0.37% muscle growth); higher vs lower volume categories differed by 3.9% (ES diff 0.241, P = 0.03); "graded dose-response relationship" — [Schoenfeld 2017, J Sports Sci (PubMed 27433992)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/); secondary: 10+ sets/week > 5–9 > <5 — [Stronger By Science summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=56687)
- Pelland et al. (preprint, 67 studies): hypertrophy and strength both rise with weekly sets (posterior probability 100%), but "diminishing returns were much stronger for strength than for size"; strength plateaus earlier — [Pelland preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [Biolayne summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-31/the-king-of-volume-metas/)
- Androulakis-Korakakis, Fisher & Steele (2020), 6 studies in resistance-trained men: "a single set of 6–12 repetitions with loads ranging from approximately 70–85% 1RM 2–3 times per week with high intensity of effort ... for 8–12 weeks can produce suboptimal, yet significant increases in SQ and BP 1RM strength" (pooled +12.09 kg overall 1RM; +17.48 kg squat; +8.25 kg bench) — [Androulakis-Korakakis 2020, Sports Med](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-minimum-effective-training-dose-required-for-1rm-strength-in-/)
- Androulakis-Korakakis et al. (2021), powerlifters: METD = ~3–6 working sets of 1–5 reps per lift per week across 1–3 sessions, >80% 1RM at RPE 7.5–9.5 for 6–12 weeks; accessory work 1–3 exercises at RPE 7–9, 6–10 reps — [Frontiers Sports Act Living 2021](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/text)
- Practitioner (Renaissance Periodization): Maintenance Volume ≈ 6 working sets/muscle/week (when each muscle is trained ≥2×/week); for beginners MEV sits near MV and they "experience rapid growth responses"; within a block add 1 set/week in good conditions, 2–3 in optimal, hold when struggling, deload to MV when performance drops — [RP: Training Volume Landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth). Secondary summaries put RP MEV at ~6–10 and MAV at ~12–20 sets/week for most muscles — [arvo.guru summary of RP](https://arvo.guru/resources/volume-landmarks)

Load / rep range
- Lopez et al. (2021) network meta-analysis, 28 studies / 747 healthy adults: strength gain superior with high (>80% 1RM) and moderate load vs low load; hypertrophy load-independent overall, with larger hypertrophy effects "in untrained participants" — [Lopez 2021, MSSE](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929)
- Schoenfeld et al. (2017) low- vs high-load meta-analysis: trend for strength (and to a lesser extent hypertrophy) to favor heavy loads — [PubMed 28834797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)
- Iversen (2021): "6–15 RM loading range (15–40 repetitions can be used if training is performed to volitional failure)"; heavy loads preferred when time is short; rest 1–2 min for untrained, ≥2 min for trained — [Iversen 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)
- Landmark (ACSM 2009): novices — load corresponding to an 8–12 RM, 2–3 days/week — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/ACSM-Position-Stand-Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf); secondary — [IDEA summary](https://www.ideafit.com/progression-models-in-resistance-training-for-healthy-adults)

Proximity to failure (RIR/RPE)
- Robinson et al. (2024), Sports Med meta-regression: RIR slope for strength contained the null ("Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR"), while hypertrophy "increased as sets were terminated closer to failure"; authors stress "modest quality of overall fit" and caution — [Robinson 2024](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/); press summary: stop ~3–5 reps short for strength — [FAU news](https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study)
- Wu et al. (2026) meta-analysis, 20 studies / 556 participants: non-failure training slightly better for dynamic strength (SMD 0.24, 95% CI 0.06–0.42, p = 0.01); no difference for hypertrophy, isometric strength, endurance, power; "reaching muscular failure is not necessary" — [Wu 2026, BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil (Europe PMC search)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22repetition%20non-failure%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- Vasconcelos et al. (2026), within-subject, 8 weeks, trained adults: 1–3 RIR produced hypertrophy and 1RM gains equivalent to failure (thickness diff 0.032 cm; 1RM diff 0.59 kg; HDIs spanning zero) — [Vasconcelos 2026, Muscles (Europe PMC search)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22repetitions-in-reserve%22%20AND%20AUTH:%22Refalo%20MC%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- RIR accuracy review (Russo et al. 2025): accuracy is greater close to failure and in upper-limb exercises; it decreases as reps increase and load decreases — [Russo 2025 review (PDF)](https://dspace.uevora.pt/rdpc/bitstream/10174/39438/1/2025%20Russo%20et%20al.%20Factors%20influencing%20the%20accuracy%20of%20the%20repetition%20in%20reserve%20scale%20in%20resistance%20training%20%20a%20systematic%20review.pdf)

### Inferences
- A sensible 12-week returner dose: start ~4–6 hard sets per muscle group/week in weeks 1–2 (near the Iversen floor; at or just above RP's "maintenance" level), build to ~8–12 by weeks 6–10. This sits where the Schoenfeld 2017 curve is still steep and well below where diminishing returns dominate.
- For returners with low RIR-estimation skill, a fixed rep target with 2–3 RIR ("stop with 2–3 clean reps left") plus moderate loads (8–15 reps) captures nearly all of the strength benefit and most of the hypertrophy benefit while limiting soreness/injury exposure; RIR accuracy is poorest exactly where it would be used most (high-rep, light sets), so keep sets ≤ ~15 reps when asking for RIR.
- Rest 90–120 s between sets for this population (Iversen's untrained recommendation), longer for heavy compound lifts.

### Gaps
- No dose-response meta-analysis stratifies specifically for "formerly trained but currently detrained" adults; minimal-dose studies are in either true novices or currently trained men.
- The autoregulation meta-analysis (Hickmott 2022) explicitly included no untrained/novice participants, so RIR/RPE effectiveness in returners is extrapolated.
- Could not retrieve the full ACSM 2009 PDF text; novice numbers (8–12 RM, 2–3 d/wk, 2–10% increments) come from secondary summaries of that document.

## Key Question 3: Progression schemes for beginners/returners (linear, double progression, RPE/RIR autoregulation); rate of load increase; plateaus

### Takeaway
In untrained people, progressing by load or by repetitions over 10 weeks produces the same strength and size gains; the ACSM's landmark rule is to add 2–10% load when the target reps can be exceeded by 1–2. RPE/RIR autoregulation matches percentage-based loading for strength in trained lifters (no novice data); its practical value for returners is as a "bad-day" governor rather than a performance booster.

### Cited Findings
- Chaves et al. (2024), 39 untrained young adults, 10 weeks, within-subject: load progression vs repetition progression — leg-extension 1RM rose ~52 → 69 kg (load) vs ~52 → 67 kg (reps); vastus lateralis CSA ~21 → 23.5 cm² in both; "progression of overload through load or repetitions can be used to promote gains in strength and muscle hypertrophy ... in the early stages of training" — [Chaves 2024, Int J Sports Med (PubMed 38286426)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38286426/)
- Plotkin et al. (2022), 43 trained adults, 8 weeks: rectus femoris growth "modestly favored REPS" (+2.8 mm adjusted), dynamic strength "slightly favored LOAD (2.0 kg [-2.4, 7.8])"; both "viable approaches" — [Plotkin 2022, PeerJ (PubMed 36199287)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)
- Landmark (ACSM 2009): "2–10% increase in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number" — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/ACSM-Position-Stand-Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf); [IDEA summary](https://www.ideafit.com/progression-models-in-resistance-training-for-healthy-adults)
- Hickmott et al. (2022) meta-analysis (15 studies; 133 participants in load-autoregulation arm, all resistance-trained): RPE/RIR-based load vs percentage-based — strength MD 2.07 kg, p = 0.09, SMD 0.21 (no significant difference; subjective autoregulation near-significant p = 0.06 in its favor); hypertrophy data insufficient (one study). Velocity-loss ≤25% better for strength (SMD 0.23), >25% better for hypertrophy (SMD 0.28). "no untrained or novice populations were included" — [Hickmott 2022, PMC8762534](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8762534/)
- Greenway/Helms et al. (2020), "Autoregulation in Resistance Training: Addressing the Inconsistencies" — conceptual review of RPE/RIR and velocity-based autoregulation — [PMC7575491](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7575491/)
- Androulakis-Korakakis (2021): autoregulated single-rep sets at RPE 9–9.5 are a minimal strategy but "strength gains will be less likely to be meaningful" — [Frontiers 2021](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/text)
- RIR accuracy improves near failure and in upper-body lifts; worse at high reps/light loads (Russo 2025) — [Russo 2025](https://dspace.uevora.pt/rdpc/bitstream/10174/39438/1/2025%20Russo%20et%20al.%20Factors%20influencing%20the%20accuracy%20of%20the%20repetition%20in%20reserve%20scale%20in%20resistance%20training%20%20a%20systematic%20review.pdf)
- Williams et al. (2017): among untrained participants, strength gains were larger with higher training frequency and longer study duration — [Williams 2017, Sports Med](https://www.springermedicine.com/comparison-of-periodized-and-non-periodized-resistance-training-/22255106)
- Practitioner (Biolayne REPS) review of "progress in load or reps for growth": both work; increasing reps is as effective as increasing load for hypertrophy in untrained people — [Biolayne REPS issue 39](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-39/progress-in-load-or-reps-for-growth/)

### Inferences
- Double progression (rep range such as 8–12; add load by the smallest available increment, ~2–10%, once the top of the range is hit on all sets with ~2 RIR) is the scheme best supported for this population: it is explicitly validated in untrained adults over exactly the program length in question (Chaves 2024), it tolerates small home-gym increments (dumbbells in 5-lb steps are ~5–10% jumps at 50–100 lb), and it self-regulates — a bad day simply yields fewer reps rather than a failed load.
- Pure linear progression (add load every session regardless of reps) relies on week-to-week neural gains that are largest in weeks 1–6 of a novice/returner; the ACSM 2–10% rule is conditional ("when the individual can perform ... one to two repetitions over"), i.e., it is itself a rep-gated progression. Expect linear load jumps to stall in upper-body pressing first, since absolute increments are a larger % of the load.
- RPE/RIR autoregulation should be layered as a cap ("stop at 2–3 RIR") and as a readiness modifier ("if the warm-up feels heavy, repeat last session's load") rather than as the primary load-selection rule for returners, because its accuracy is poorest in the high-rep, light-load sets novices use.
- Plateau handling with evidence support is thin; the only directly tested levers are (i) switching from load progression to rep progression or vice versa (both work), (ii) adding sets (dose-response), and (iii) brief deloads (see Q4; evidence does not show a benefit).

### Gaps
- No study directly compares linear vs double progression vs RPE-based progression in detrained/returning adults; evidence is from untrained young adults (Chaves) and trained adults (Plotkin, Hickmott).
- No evidence-based number for "how fast load should rise per week" beyond the ACSM 2–10% conditional rule; novice strength gains in the first 4–8 weeks are dominated by neural/skill factors, so load trajectories in trials are not reported as weekly % targets.
- No controlled trial on plateau-breaking strategies in novices.

## Key Question 4: Periodization over 10–12 weeks; deloads; detraining and retraining ("muscle memory")

### Takeaway
Periodized programs outperform non-periodized ones for 1RM strength by a small-to-moderate margin (ES ~0.3–0.4), with no hypertrophy difference and essentially no advantage of undulating over linear in beginners; much of the "periodization" effect may just reflect reaching heavier loads. A pre-planned one-week complete rest deload mid-program did not help hypertrophy and slightly hurt strength in trained lifters. Formerly trained people regain lost strength and size markedly faster than naive trainees — on the order of 5–8 weeks of retraining to recover a 12–20-week layoff's losses — and strength losses after detraining are modest (~5–15%, with ~60% of the strength gain retained after 20 weeks).

### Cited Findings
Periodization
- Williams et al. (2017), 18 studies / 81 effects: periodized > non-periodized for 1RM, ES 0.43 (95% CI 0.27–0.58; P < 0.001); undulating programs superior; untrained participants gained more with higher frequency and longer duration — [Williams 2017, Sports Med](https://www.springermedicine.com/comparison-of-periodized-and-non-periodized-resistance-training-/22255106)
- Volume-equated meta-analysis (2022; 35 studies): periodized favored for 1RM, ES 0.31 (95% CI 0.04–0.57; P = 0.02); negligible hypertrophy effect (ES ~0.13) — [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/); summary — [sci-sport.com](https://sci-sport.com/en/effects-intensity-volume-periodization-strength-hypertrophy-238/)
- Undulating vs linear for maximal strength: ES 0.31 overall, 0.61 in trained but only 0.06 in beginners — [sci-sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/effects-intensity-volume-periodization-strength-hypertrophy-238/)
- Grgic et al. (2017), 13 studies: linear vs daily-undulating periodization, hypertrophy d = −0.02 (95% CI −0.25 to 0.21, p = 0.848) — "likely to be similar" — [Grgic 2017, PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/3695/)
- Caveat (practitioner, Stronger By Science): periodized groups typically train at higher peak intensities, so trials may be testing peak intensity rather than periodization per se — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=52473)

Deloads
- Coleman et al. (2024), PeerJ RCT, 39 trained adults (~21–22 y), 9 weeks, upper/lower 4×/week, 5 sets × 8–12 to failure: one-week complete cessation at week 5 vs continuous training — hypertrophy "no meaningful between-group differences"; strength favored continuous training (1RM squat posterior probability 0.851; isometric knee extension 0.924); authors say complete cessation "likely should be used more sparingly" when strength is the priority and that "autoregulated deloads" may be superior — [Coleman 2024, PeerJ (PMC10809978)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/)
- Practitioner (RP): deload to maintenance volume (~6 sets/muscle) when performance drops, typically at the end of a 4–6-week accumulation block — [RP volume landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)

Detraining and retraining
- Psilander et al. (2019), 19 adults, 10 wk unilateral training → 20 wk detraining → 5 wk retraining: training raised CSA ~17%, muscle thickness ~10%, strength ~20%; after 20 wk detraining strength "remained elevated (~60%)" of the gain; retraining responses were similar in previously trained and naive legs, and no myonuclear "memory" was found — [Psilander 2019, J Appl Physiol](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1336656/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- Blocquiaux et al. (2020), 30 older men, 12 wk training / ~12 wk detraining / 12 wk retraining: training +10 to +36% strength and power; detraining −5 to −15% strength/power, −17% type II CSA trend; "Less than eight weeks of retraining were needed to reach the post-training level of one-repetition maximum strength"; retraining +29% type II fiber CSA — [Blocquiaux 2020, Exp Gerontol (Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22Blocquiaux%20S%22%20AND%20TITLE:detraining&resultType=core&format=json)
- Periodic vs continuous training (Ogasawara-type design, summarized): the periodic group lost CSA and strength during detraining but "regained these losses rapidly," gaining faster than the continuous group during the first 5 weeks of retraining — [biolayne summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-29/decoding-muscle-memory-insights-for-gains/)
- Myonuclei gained from 10 weeks of training persist after 16 weeks of detraining (one of the pro-"muscle memory" findings; conflicts with Psilander 2019) — [NPR/WBHM summary](https://wbhm.org/npr-story/after-a-break-from-strength-training-muscle-memory-may-help-you-bounce-back)
- Landmark (Bickel et al. 2011), 70 adults, 16 wk training then 32 wk detraining or 1/3 or 1/9 maintenance dose: in young adults (20–35) both maintenance doses preserved hypertrophy; in older adults (60–75) neither did; "Strength gained during phase 1 was largely retained throughout detraining with only a slight reduction at the final time point" — [Bickel 2011, MSSE (PubMed 21131862)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/)

### Inferences
- For a 10–12-week returner program, a simple two-phase structure (a 2-week "re-entry" at low volume/effort, then an 8–10-week build with a rep-range shift or small load emphasis change) is consistent with the periodization evidence: it gives the modest strength advantage of planned intensity progression without needing undulation, which shows ~zero extra benefit in beginners (ES 0.06).
- A fixed, pre-scheduled rest week is not supported by the one RCT; a better-supported design is an autoregulated or "soft" deload (cut sets ~50%, keep loads) triggered by missed sessions, poor performance or persistent soreness. Because the product's philosophy already treats missed days as neutral, missed days effectively serve as unplanned mini-deloads.
- Formerly trained adults should expect to approach prior strength levels within roughly the first 6–8 weeks and should not be programmed as true novices in load tolerance — but their connective tissue and soreness tolerance have decayed, so the early ramp should be conservative on volume and effort, not on skill or load complexity.

### Gaps
- No trial tests a "2-week re-entry then build" phase structure specifically; this is inferred from periodization and repeated-bout literature.
- Deload evidence is from one RCT in young trained lifters; no deload RCT exists in deconditioned 30–55-year-olds. A registered untrained-population trial (NCT06825052) was completed in 2024 but results were not found — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06825052](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06825052)
- Human "muscle memory" mechanism is contested (Psilander 2019 found no myonuclear retention effect; other work reports retained myonuclei); the faster-regain phenomenon is better established than its mechanism.
- Retraining data come from 5–8-week layoffs after lab training, not multi-year layoffs typical of returners; retraining speed after years off is not directly measured.

## Key Question 5: Injury and soreness risk when ramping up after a layoff; recommended ramp rates

### Takeaway
Conventional strength training is among the safest physical activities (≈0.24–1 injury per 1,000 training hours); soreness risk is front-loaded into the first exposure to each movement and is sharply reduced after a single, even low-volume, bout (repeated bout effect). No RCT defines a ramp rate for resistance training; the defensible approach is a deliberately sub-maximal first 1–2 weeks (few sets, 3+ RIR, familiar movements) then volume/effort increases of roughly one set per muscle per week and load jumps of 2–10% only when reps allow.

### Cited Findings
- Keogh & Winwood (2017) systematic review: bodybuilding-style training had the lowest injury rates, "0.12–0.7 injuries per lifter per year; 0.24–1 injury per 1000 h"; strongman 4.5–6.1 and Highland Games 7.5 per 1000 h; most common sites shoulder, lower back, knee, elbow, wrist/hand; strains, tendinitis, sprains predominate — [Keogh & Winwood 2017, Sports Med (Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22Keogh%20JW%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22weight-training%20sports%22&resultType=core&format=json); NSCA PTQ summary — [NSCA: Safest and Riskiest Forms of RT](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/ptq/the-safest-and-riskiest-forms-of-resistance-training/)
- 2023 systematic review (J Orthop Surg Res): strength training <1 injury/1000 h; weightlifting 3.2; powerlifting 4; strongman 5.5; CrossFit 1.94–3.1 — "traditional strength training is the safest resistance training method" — [JOSR 2023](https://josr-online.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13018-023-03781-x)
- Repeated bout effect: a single bout of unaccustomed exercise protects against damage/soreness in later bouts for weeks; "exercise does not need to cause damage to have protective effect" and "the volume of contractions can be significantly lower than a subsequent bout and still provide protection," though contraction intensity near maximal in the first bout produces the strongest protection; a workout on one limb also protects the other — [Hyldahl, Chen & Nosaka 2017, ESSR (Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22Hyldahl%20RD%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22repeated%20bout%22&resultType=core&format=json); [ECU record, muscle-specific variations](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/991); practitioner summary — [Biolayne DOMS article](https://biolayne.com/articles/training/doms-repeated-bout-effect/)
- Contralateral repeated bout effect (Xin et al. 2014): second eccentric bout 4 weeks later showed smaller strength loss at 24–96 h and attenuated NF-κB inflammatory signalling — [Xin 2014, J Appl Physiol (Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22Xin%20L%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22contralateral%20repeated%20bout%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- Landmark (ACSM 2009): load increments of 2–10% only when 1–2 extra reps can be done — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/ACSM-Position-Stand-Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf)
- Practitioner (Stronger By Science): when adding exposure, reduce per-session volume and keep an "easy" session "at least 3–4 reps from failure" — [SBS training frequency](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/training-frequency/)
- Practitioner (RP): add ~1 set/muscle/week under normal recovery; hold or deload when performance or soreness worsens — [RP volume landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)
- Non-failure training was at least as effective as failure training and better for dynamic strength (SMD 0.24) — relevant because failure training maximizes fatigue/soreness without benefit (Wu 2026) — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22repetition%20non-failure%22&resultType=core&format=json)

### Inferences
- The highest-leverage soreness control is sequencing: expose every movement pattern once at low volume and effort in week 1 (e.g., 1–2 sets each, 3–4 RIR) so the repeated-bout effect is "banked" before volume rises. Eccentric-heavy or novel exercises (lunges, RDLs, Nordic-type work) deserve the most conservative first exposure.
- Injury risk in conventional strength training is low enough that the main practical concern for returners is soreness-driven dropout and tendon/connective-tissue overuse at the shoulder, low back and knee — the sites Keogh & Winwood identify. Keeping heavy compound lifts to moderate loads (8–12+ reps) and 2–3 RIR in the first month addresses both.
- A workable ramp heuristic consistent with the sources: weeks 1–2 at ~50–60% of target weekly sets and ≥3 RIR; add ~1 set per muscle per week thereafter; load +2–10% only when the rep target is exceeded; never increase sets and load in the same week for the same lift. This is a synthesis, not a tested protocol.

### Gaps
- No RCT or cohort study quantifies injury or soreness rates as a function of ramp rate in resistance training; the "10% rule" has running-injury origins and no RT evidence was found.
- No specific data on injury rates in 30–55-year-olds returning after a multi-year layoff versus true novices.
- Repeated-bout data come mostly from lab eccentric protocols, not typical whole-body gym sessions.

## Key Question 6: Exercise selection principles — compound movement-pattern templates; generalization across equipment

### Takeaway
Guidelines and time-efficiency reviews converge on prioritizing bilateral multi-joint lifts covering at minimum a leg press/squat pattern, an upper push and an upper pull; single-joint add-ons contribute little. Load-independence of hypertrophy and the low importance of implement choice in the evidence base support treating patterns (squat, hinge, push, pull, carry, single-leg, core) as the template and filling them with whatever equipment is available; direct trials on pattern-templates across equipment are lacking.

### Cited Findings
- Iversen et al. (2021): minimum program is "at least one leg pressing exercise (e.g. squats), one upper-body pulling exercise (e.g. pull-up) and one upper-body pushing exercise (e.g. bench press)," "prioritizing bilateral, multi-joint exercises that include full dynamic movements"; "unlikely that the inclusion of single-joint exercises will meaningfully contribute to additional short- or long-term benefits"; time-saving techniques (supersets, drop sets, rest-pause) "roughly halve training time" but are not advised for heavy free-weight compound lifts — [Iversen 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)
- Grgic et al. (2018): frequency effects on strength were present for multi-joint exercises but not single-joint, suggesting compound lifts are the skill-dependent, practice-responsive part of a program — [Grgic 2018](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9)
- Lopez et al. (2021): hypertrophy is load-independent across 28 studies (so lighter implements such as dumbbells/kettlebells/bands can substitute for barbells when taken close enough to failure), while strength favors heavier loads — [Lopez 2021](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929)
- Landmark (ACSM 2009): novice recommendations emphasize multi-joint exercises, free weights and machines, 2–3 whole-body days/week, 8–12 RM — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/ACSM-Position-Stand-Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf)
- Practitioner (NSCA PTQ 10.2.1): fundamental patterns — squat (knee-dominant), hinge (hip-dominant), lunge, horizontal/vertical push, horizontal/vertical pull, carry, jump/land; "Competency in these patterns is needed for effectiveness, standardization, and safety of comprehensive resistance training programs" — [NSCA PTQ 10.2.1](https://dxpprod.nsca.com/contentassets/3d09f06f0b4c4f6fbd8cc382ed1f3d4a/ptq-10.2.1-progressive-strategies-for-teaching-fundamental-resistance-training-movement-patterns.pdf); coaching framing — [World Rugby movement patterns](https://passport.world.rugby/conditioning-for-rugby/advanced-conditioning-for-rugby-pre-level-2/resistance-training/the-gym-based-training-programme/movement-patterns/)
- Rios et al. (2026) narrative review of RT methods/systems: adaptations "are primarily governed by foundational prescription variables, including load, volume, frequency, effort, density, and exercise selection," with advanced methods showing no consistent superiority — [Rios 2026, Sports (Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22Methods%20and%20Systems%20of%20Resistance%20Training%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- Androulakis-Korakakis (2021): accessory work of 1–3 exercises per main lift at RPE 7–9, 6–10 reps is sufficient alongside minimal main-lift dosing — [Frontiers 2021](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/text)

### Inferences
- A 3-day full-body template built as [squat or single-leg] + [hinge] + [horizontal or vertical push] + [horizontal or vertical pull] + optional [carry/core] per session, with A/B variation, covers the Iversen minimum several times per week and lets each slot be filled by goblet squat, landmine press, kettlebell RDL, dumbbell row, etc. Because hypertrophy is load-independent and strength depends on effort and progressive load rather than the implement, the template should generalize across home, gym and minimal-equipment settings — this is a reasoned extrapolation, not a tested claim.
- Single-joint and "finisher" work is optional: include it for preference and adherence, not for outcomes, and schedule it after the compound slots so it can be dropped when time is short.
- Supersetting non-competing patterns (push + pull, or upper + lower) is an evidence-supported way to shorten sessions for time-constrained returners, as long as the heaviest compound lift is done straight-set.

### Gaps
- No trial directly tests whether a "pattern-based template" produces equivalent outcomes when the same patterns are loaded with different equipment (barbell vs dumbbell vs cable vs bodyweight). Free-weight vs machine comparisons exist in the literature but were not retrieved in this search.
- No evidence base specifically for carries or single-leg work as required template slots; their inclusion rests on practitioner consensus (NSCA, coaching sources), not outcome trials.
- The NSCA Essentials textbook was not retrievable online; the NSCA PTQ article is the closest primary NSCA source found.
